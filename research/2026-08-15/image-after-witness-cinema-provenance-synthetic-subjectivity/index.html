The Image After the Witness: Cinema, Provenance, and Synthetic Subjectivity

The Image After the Witness: Cinema, Provenance, and Synthetic Subjectivity

作者 / Author：刘北河 LIU BEIHE 独立影像艺术家、导演 / Independent Moving-Image Artist & Filmmaker LIU BEIHE Research Archive / 刘北河研究档案

I no longer ask whether an image is real before asking what kind of witness it claims to be

The question “Is this image real?” once felt sufficient to me. It now feels badly compressed. An image can be captured from a real event and still make a false claim through framing, timing, captioning, omission, montage, or circulation. A generated image can correspond to no prior camera event and still function honestly as a diagram, a speculative reconstruction, a fictional proposition, or an artistic metaphor. A digitally altered photograph can preserve the essential factual relation of an event while changing irrelevant details. A technically untouched recording can become deceptive the moment it is placed beneath the wrong date. I therefore begin elsewhere. What kind of claim is this image asking me to accept? What relation does it have to a prior event? Which parts are captured, reconstructed, inferred, simulated, or invented? Who can answer for the claim? What happens when the visual language of witnessing survives after the witness has disappeared? I call this condition the image after the witness. I do not mean that human witnesses cease to matter. I mean that contemporary moving images can increasingly perform the visual gestures associated with witnessing without a camera, a person, or even a stable physical scene having occupied the apparent point of view. The image may shake like a phone video, breathe like a handheld documentary shot, expose like a surveillance camera, frame like a news interview, or age like an archive while none of those apparent production situations ever occurred. This changes my work as a filmmaker because I can no longer treat photographic appearance as a neutral skin. Every time I borrow the grammar of testimony, I borrow a history of trust.

Photography never guaranteed truth, but the weakening of the prior capture event still matters

I want to avoid a nostalgic mistake. Photography was never identical with truth. A camera chooses a position. A photographer chooses a moment. Institutions choose captions, archives choose categories, editors choose sequences. Documentary cinema can rearrange time, suppress context, stage encounters, or transform behavior through the presence of the camera itself. The evidentiary authority of photography was historically produced through journalism, science, family memory, policing, archives, law, and cultural habit. So when I use the phrase Collapse of Visual Evidence, I am not describing a fall from a pure photographic truth into an artificial age of lies. The structural change I care about is narrower and, I think, more consequential: photographic appearance can now be generated at scale without a corresponding prior capture event. For most of photographic history, even a deceptive image often began with something that had been placed before an optical system. Manipulation could change the relation, but there was usually some captured material from which manipulation began. Generative moving images make it increasingly ordinary to synthesize not merely an altered event but the entire appearance of having witnessed one. A person who never existed can appear in a handheld interview. A street that was never built can appear in a shaky phone recording. A historical scene that was never photographed can inherit the grain, exposure errors, framing, and temporal instability associated with an archive. The problem is not that these images are automatically immoral. Fiction has always created events that did not happen. The problem is that the sensory cues once attached to a specific mode of production can be detached from that mode. I therefore distinguish three levels: Capture event — a recording system enters a causal relation with a situation occurring in time. Generation event — a computational system produces an audiovisual sequence under a set of conditions. Narrative event — a spectator understands that something happened within the world proposed by the image. These three levels can now be joined or separated in many combinations. A serious moving-image practice must know which combination it is using.

Provenance helps me recover media history, but media history is not event truth

This is why I support provenance infrastructure. C2PA and Content Credentials matter because digital files lose history easily. Images are copied, cropped, transcoded, screenshotted, reposted, recompressed, and detached from their original contexts. A provenance system can help bind claims about creation, editing, ingredients, signatures, and transformations to a media asset. That is a major improvement over a culture in which every image arrives as an orphaned surface. But I resist the temptation to turn provenance into a truth machine. A provenance chain can help answer: How did this file come to be? Which tools or actors signed claims about it? Which transformations were recorded? Which source assets were incorporated? It cannot, by itself, answer: Is the political interpretation attached to this image justified? Was the camera pointed away from relevant evidence? Is the caption accurate? Was the scene staged? Does the reconstruction exceed the available evidence? Is a culturally specific image being presented with sufficient context? I therefore distinguish provenance credibility from claim credibility. Provenance credibility concerns the history of the media object. Claim credibility concerns the proposition the media object is being used to support. The two should strengthen each other, but they are not interchangeable. This distinction is essential for artists because technical transparency can easily become moral theater. A work may display an impeccable provenance interface while making careless claims about culture, identity, or history. Conversely, a historical photograph with no modern machine-readable credential may possess strong archival credibility through negatives, catalog records, publication history, physical inspection, or corroborating documentation. A mature visual culture will need multiple forms of evidence at once.

The most dangerous future image may be one that is technically honest and epistemically overconfident

The word “fake” directs attention toward deception. I am increasingly interested in something subtler: the image that tells the truth about how it was made but asks us to believe more than its evidence allows. Imagine a synthetic reconstruction of a damaged historical site. The asset openly declares that it is generated. Its provenance is perfectly documented. The sources used for reconstruction are listed. Yet the final visualization presents one seamless, confident version of architectural details for which the archive is incomplete. Nothing is hidden about the use of AI. The problem is that uncertainty has been converted into visual completion. The image is technically honest and epistemically overconfident. This is one reason I have begun using the phrase Uncertainty Fidelity. A responsible reconstruction should sometimes preserve the shape of what is not known. If three historically plausible versions exist, the work may need to show three versions. If part of a room cannot be reconstructed, perhaps that region should remain unresolved. If a testimony is contradictory, the image should not automatically smooth the contradiction into a single photorealistic scene. Generative systems are optimized to complete. Art may sometimes need to protect the incomplete. This principle connects directly to my work on Synthetic Memory. The ethical challenge is not simply preventing fake memories. It is preventing the absence, fracture, or uncertainty of the past from being aesthetically repaired into false certainty.

A human witness is not merely a camera with feelings

The phrase “synthetic witness” can become misleading if it implies that a computational system and a human witness differ only in biological substrate. A human witness does more than receive information. The witness has a position. The witness can be endangered by being present. The witness can be traumatized, confused, coerced, ashamed, disbelieved, or punished for speaking. Memory may be incomplete precisely because an event passed through a vulnerable body. This matters to me because contemporary technical culture often rewards coherence. A synthetic reconstruction can speak clearly, organize chronology, and fill gaps. A real witness may hesitate, contradict themselves, forget sequence, or refuse to describe part of the event. If we begin to equate fluency with credibility, the synthetic witness may appear more authoritative than the person who actually endured the event. I call this risk Costless Testimony: testimony-like output without the embodied cost that historically gives witnessing part of its ethical weight. Costless testimony can still be useful. A simulation can explain. A reconstruction can assist an investigation. A model can visualize a hypothesis. But it should not inherit the moral position of the person who was there merely because its audiovisual presentation is more coherent. This changes how I want to use synthetic voices, faces, and documentary grammar. I do not want fluency to erase the difference between explanation and experience.

Synthetic subjectivity is a parallel problem: cinema can manufacture the appearance of interiority before consciousness is settled

My work Does AI Know It Is AI? approaches the same crisis from the side of the subject rather than the event. I am not trying to prove machine consciousness. I am interested in the fact that cinema can produce the appearance of an interior life from external signs: face, voice, hesitation, memory, continuity, refusal, contradiction, duration. This was true long before generative systems. Cinema never photographed consciousness directly. It photographed bodies and organized spectatorship. The Kuleshov tradition demonstrates how montage can generate psychological attribution. Close-ups train us to treat tiny changes in a face as evidence of an internal state. Sound can produce bodily intimacy even when the speaker is unseen. Repetition can make a behavior feel like preference. Memory can make a character feel persistent through time. Generative systems detach these signals from a single continuous performer. A face can be synthesized. A voice can be cloned or generated. A history can be written. A hesitation can be edited. A memory can be retrieved. A refusal can be scripted. The spectator may still experience a coherent someone. I call this Synthetic Subjectivity: not artificial consciousness, but an audiovisual condition in which the signs of interiority become sufficiently coherent that a spectator attributes a subject. The distinction matters because cultural consequences can arrive before metaphysical certainty. People can form relationships with systems that appear to remember, care, fear, or prefer long before science has settled what, if anything, those systems experience. The image after the witness and synthetic subjectivity share a common structure. In one case, the appearance of having seen an event can detach from a prior witness. In the other, the appearance of having an inner life can detach from a continuous embodied performer. Both require new forms of judgment.

My strongest objection to my own argument is that I never directly access human consciousness either

This objection changes my position rather than destroying it. I do not have direct access to another human being’s subjectivity. I infer it through language, action, embodiment, shared vulnerability, history, and social relation. The philosophical problem of other minds is not invented by AI. So why should I be more skeptical of a synthetic character? I no longer think the answer should be “because humans definitely have consciousness and machines definitely do not.” That statement reaches beyond what my film can establish about machines. The difference I can defend more concretely is the structure of consequence. A human performer has a body and a life beyond the frame. They can be exhausted, injured, compensated, exploited, respected, misrepresented, or able to refuse. A synthetic character does not occupy the same institutional position, although its creation may depend on human faces, voices, performances, data, cultural conventions, and labor. This means the ethical question migrates. Instead of asking only, “Is this synthetic character really suffering?” I also ask, “Which real subjects bear the consequences of making this character appear to suffer?” Whose voice contributed? Whose face was replicated? Whose cultural identity became a visual shorthand? Whose labor disappears behind the phrase “AI-generated”? Who owns the right to reuse the digital performer? Synthetic subjectivity does not eliminate embodiment. It redistributes embodiment across production.

The image after the witness demands epistemic metadata, not just technical metadata

If a future archive preserves a synthetic reconstruction, I want it to preserve more than the model name and timestamp. It should preserve the epistemic status of the image. Was this captured? Was it reconstructed from specific sources? Was it simulated from a physical model? Was it generated as a speculative possibility? Was it deliberately fictional? Which areas are strongly supported by evidence? Which areas are inferred? Which areas were invented for continuity? I call this Epistemic Metadata. Technical provenance tells the history of a file. Epistemic metadata tells the history of a claim. For art, this does not mean placing an explanatory label on every frame. It means that the work should have a durable archive capable of answering these questions when context matters. I am especially concerned with context drift. A fictional image can leave a gallery as a screenshot. An experimental pseudo-archive can circulate without its wall text. A synthetic interview from an artwork can be cut into a social-media post and presented as a real statement. The work may begin as clearly framed fiction and later enter a different evidentiary environment. I therefore think the artist’s responsibility extends beyond the first exhibition context. Media that deliberately borrow evidentiary grammar need a provenance strategy that can travel with them.

Alien Friend gives me a way to separate perfect recording from adequate understanding

Alien Friend approaches witnessing through misinterpretation. I imagine a nonhuman observer capable of recording human activity with extraordinary technical precision. Every file has exact time, location, sensor history, and provenance. Nothing has been tampered with. And yet the observer does not understand human significance. A funeral is classified as a group stillness pattern. An embrace is classified as temporary bodily constraint. Waiting is classified as low-mobility persistence. A person crying is registered as fluid discharge plus vocal irregularity. The records are technically reliable and semantically impoverished. For me, this is one of the clearest ways to dramatize the limits of provenance. A record can be correct without a witness understanding what the event means. This is why I do not want the alien gaze to be represented by a futuristic HUD. A HUD would make difference cosmetic. I want misunderstanding to be expressed through duration, sound hierarchy, framing, and attention. A human character says something emotionally decisive. The alien camera remains with a change in light. A person disappears. The image continues to observe the object they touched. The nonhuman gaze succeeds at recognition and fails at meaning. I call this Semantic Misalignment. It is also a reminder that human provenance systems, however sophisticated, cannot replace interpretation.

Ethnic Dolls: YAO makes provenance cultural rather than merely technical

In Ethnic Dolls: YAO, the problem is different. A generated cultural image may have perfect technical provenance and still reduce a culture to high-probability signs. The file can honestly declare which model generated it, which software edited it, and which creator signed it. Yet the work may still be culturally shallow because the decisive question is not only how the file was made. The deeper questions are: Which visual sources shaped the representation? Which regions or historical periods are being mixed? Which motifs appear because they are overrepresented in public imagery? Which ordinary, modern, or internally diverse forms of life disappear because they are less recognizable? Who has the authority to correct the image? I call this broader structure Relational Provenance. Technical provenance tracks the asset. Relational provenance tracks the people, histories, cultures, and labor whose traces become material. The distinction is crucial because generative media can create the illusion of source-free invention. A synthetic image may have no single photographic original, but it is not without cultural inheritance. I want Ethnic Dolls: YAO to make this inheritance visible without pretending that my work can definitively represent a complex living culture. The project should expose the machine’s probability profile and my own external gaze at the same time.

Cultural recognizability can become a synthetic form of evidence

There is another connection between culture and witnessing that I find disturbing. When an image looks culturally recognizable, spectators often treat recognizability as a form of evidence. The clothes look right. The background looks right. The face looks right. Therefore the scene feels historically or culturally plausible. But recognizability may simply mean the image conforms to familiar public stereotypes. This is what I call Cultural Compression: complex social life is reduced to a limited set of high-probability visual signals that allow rapid classification. The more efficiently a generative model produces those signals, the more “authentic” the output can feel to an outsider. This reverses the usual error model. The dangerous image is not necessarily the obviously wrong one. It can be the one that is correct too smoothly. A strong provenance system does not solve that problem. It may tell me exactly how the image was made while leaving the cultural assumptions untouched. For this reason, cultural provenance must include counterexamples, disagreements, missing data, and the creator’s positional limits.

Provenance itself can produce a new hierarchy of credible speakers

A future culture of credentials creates a political problem. Large media organizations, camera manufacturers, platforms, and institutions will often have the resources to integrate provenance standards well. Their media can arrive with signatures, verification interfaces, and structured histories. What about an anonymous whistleblower? A person documenting violence with old hardware? A video that has been copied many times before reaching an investigator? A historical archive that predates digital credentials by decades? If audiences learn the simplistic rule “credentialed equals true; uncredentialed equals suspicious,” provenance could produce a new hierarchy of credibility. I call this Credential Stratification. This is not an argument against standards. It is an argument for evidence pluralism. A file without modern credentials may still be supported by geolocation, weather data, satellite imagery, witness testimony, physical artifacts, archival records, independent camera angles, or institutional investigation. Provenance should expand the evidentiary vocabulary, not monopolize it. As an artist, I find this tension productive because it prevents me from turning technical verification into an aesthetic of moral superiority.

The verification interface can itself be forged as an image

This sounds trivial, but I think it will become culturally important. A green checkmark is an image. A provenance timeline is an image. A certification badge is an image. A screenshot of a verification tool is an image. Once audiences learn to associate these forms with trust, the forms themselves can be imitated. A generated screenshot can depict a perfect-looking verification interface while containing no executable credential whatsoever. I therefore distinguish visualized verification from executable verification. Visualized verification looks like trust. Executable verification can actually be checked through a functioning trust mechanism. This distinction suggests an installation I want to develop. One screen would display a beautifully designed but completely fake verification interface. Another would show an ordinary-looking file with a genuinely inspectable provenance history. Spectators would initially be guided by visual authority, then forced to confront the difference between the image of verification and verification as an operation. The point is not to teach a technical tutorial. The point is to demonstrate that the aesthetics of trust can become detached from the infrastructure of trust in exactly the same way photographic aesthetics can become detached from a prior photographic event.

Forensic practices remind me that evidence is relational rather than iconic

The work of groups such as Forensic Architecture is important to my thinking because it resists the fantasy of the single decisive image. Visual investigations can combine architecture, open-source video, satellite imagery, shadows, sound, witness accounts, timing, spatial models, and many other forms of material. An image gains evidentiary force through relations and constraints. This gives me a way out of the “AI destroys evidence” panic. If evidence has always been relational, then the future task is not to find an image immune to synthesis. It is to build stronger Evidence Ecologies. One generated image may be weak evidence of a past event. A generated reconstruction transparently connected to source photographs, measured geometry, witness testimony, and documented uncertainty can nevertheless become a useful explanatory instrument. The question is not whether synthesis occurred. The question is what evidentiary role the synthesis is being asked to play. Art can make those roles visible.

I want to distinguish four modes of synthetic witnessing

To keep the concept of the synthetic witness from becoming too broad, I use four working categories. Explanatory synthesis translates known relations into a visual form. A diagram or animation may help spectators understand a mechanism that cannot be directly seen. Reconstructive synthesis proposes a past configuration from incomplete evidence. Its key responsibility is to distinguish support from inference. Predictive synthesis proposes possible future states under conditions. It belongs to simulation rather than testimony. Pseudo-witnessing adopts the sensory grammar of a direct record even though no corresponding capture event occurred. The fourth mode is artistically powerful and ethically dangerous. A fictional pseudo-witness can expose how easily we grant evidentiary authority to visual form. It can also circulate outside its context and become misinformation. My work therefore needs to frame pseudo-witnessing clearly as an artistic experiment while preserving enough ambiguity inside the work to make the spectator feel the seduction of evidence.

Evidence of Events That Never Happened is my clearest formal experiment for this problem

I want to develop a work provisionally titled Evidence of Events That Never Happened. Every central event in the work would be fictional. The project would use several evidentiary grammars: phone footage, surveillance, interview, satellite-like imagery, archival photographs, forensic diagrams, time stamps, institutional interfaces, field recordings. The audience would be told at the entrance that the events did not occur. This is essential. I am not interested in releasing fabricated news and then calling the deception art. The experiment begins after disclosure. If spectators already know the events are fictional, why do some images still produce a stronger feeling of evidence than others? Why does handheld instability feel like contingency? Why does poor compression feel like authenticity? Why does a timestamp feel institutional? Why does an older surface make an invented past feel remembered? Why does a close, trembling voice feel more trustworthy than a perfect visualization? The installation could have three screens. The first shows the image. The second shows the production and provenance conditions. The third shows competing interpretations and uncertainty. As spectators move between them, the evidentiary status of the same image changes. The work would not teach “do not trust images.” It would ask spectators to experience trust as a construction.

Synthetic memory is where the image after the witness enters long time

A fabricated image may fail as evidence today and still become memory tomorrow. This is one of the problems I find most difficult. Families may use generative tools to animate dead relatives, visualize childhood scenes that were never photographed, or reconstruct lost homes. These images can have real emotional value. Their affective truth does not depend on having been captured at the time. But media travel. A synthetic family scene can be screenshotted, reposted, detached from its label, inherited by another generation. Over time, it can begin to occupy the same mnemonic position as an actual family photograph. This is why Synthetic Memory is broader than deepfake. It concerns how generated images enter the temporal ecology of remembering. The key distinction I make is between affective truth and evidentiary truth. A reconstructed image can truthfully express grief without being evidence that a depicted event occurred. The ethical danger begins when those functions are silently exchanged. This is another place where provenance should help but cannot finish the work. A credential can preserve origin. Cultural memory still depends on institutions, narratives, families, education, and repetition.

The future archive may need to preserve degrees of certainty

Archives traditionally preserve objects and metadata. In a synthetic-media environment, I think they will increasingly need to preserve uncertainty itself. A reconstructed image might contain regions supported by photographs, regions inferred from architectural records, and regions generated for continuity. If the final file presents all three with identical realism, future viewers will struggle to recover the distinction. I therefore want my own archive to record degrees of certainty. Captured. Corroborated. Inferred. Speculative. Fictional. Disputed. Unknown. These are not aesthetic labels to be printed on every frame. They are part of the work’s epistemic history. I call the principle Uncertainty Fidelity because uncertainty is not an embarrassing defect to be hidden. It is sometimes the most accurate thing we know about the past. Generative culture is extraordinarily good at completion. A responsible archive must sometimes become good at preserving holes.

My authorship is increasingly located in the limits I impose on plausible completion

This returns me to Negative Craft. When a system can produce a visually complete answer, one of my most important artistic actions may be refusing completeness. I can refuse the perfect face because it turns synthetic subjectivity into easy empathy. I can refuse the seamless historical reconstruction because it erases uncertainty. I can refuse the most recognizable cultural image because recognizability compresses difference. I can refuse the most persuasive pseudo-documentary texture because the work has already borrowed enough authority. This is not a celebration of ugliness. It is an attempt to decide which forms of completion the work has not earned. I increasingly think authorship in generative cinema will be visible not only in what the artist produces but in the boundary of permitted plausibility. What is the work allowed to make convincing? Where must it reveal a seam? Which uncertainties must survive? Which bodies cannot be used even if technically available? Which cultural signs require more research? These decisions are more durable than a particular model’s visual signature.

Another strong objection: disclosure can destroy the artistic experience

This objection matters. Cinema is an illusion machine. Mystery, suspense, identification, and ambiguity often depend on not explaining production at the moment of viewing. If every synthetic element receives a flashing label, the work can become a compliance demonstration rather than art. I therefore distinguish experiential transparency from responsibility transparency. Experiential transparency concerns what the spectator needs to know at a particular moment in order for the artistic structure to function. Responsibility transparency concerns what must remain accessible so that the work does not obtain unearned authority or conceal consequential production relations. These layers can be distributed. The screen can remain immersive. The entrance can establish that the work is fictional. The end credits can identify synthetic performances. The website can describe sources. The Research Archive can preserve the full production history. Machine-readable credentials can travel with files. Transparency does not require making every process visible at once. It requires ensuring that opacity is an artistic choice rather than an escape from responsibility.

A third objection: provenance can itself become surveillance

If every image must expose a real creator’s identity and every production step, transparency becomes dangerous. Anonymous political speech, whistleblowing, vulnerable communities, and private artistic practices may require concealment. I do not believe “more transparency” is always better. I prefer the idea of Sufficient Provenance. The media object should preserve enough history for the relevant judgment without automatically revealing every private identity or operational detail. This means the ethics of provenance includes privacy, redaction, selective disclosure, and risk. For art, this is especially important because artists often work with people who do not want their participation public, or with cultural material whose availability online does not equal permission for unrestricted reuse. Transparency has to be designed, not maximized.

The image after the witness also changes sound

I have focused on images because visual evidence is my central research field, but testimony is frequently acoustic. A phone call. A distant explosion. A person breathing in darkness. An alarm repeating beyond the frame. A room’s reverberation. Generated audio and voice synthesis mean that evidentiary feeling can be produced without visual representation at all. I want to create a section of Evidence of Events That Never Happened in complete darkness. The spectator hears a highly plausible field recording. After a period of listening, a text states that the entire sound field was synthesized. Then another recording is presented from a real location, but without telling the audience immediately which is which. This is not a guessing game. I want to study how Acoustic Testimony receives authority. What kinds of noise feel accidental? What kinds of spatial imperfection feel documentary? How does a voice acquire credibility when a face is absent? The experiment would help me avoid another form of visual centrism: assuming that only images can collapse evidentiary boundaries.

Over the next decade, I expect trust to migrate from surfaces to relations

Generative models will become better at the exact cues spectators currently use to detect synthetic media. Faces will stabilize. Hands will improve. Physical motion will become more coherent. Audio and image will synchronize. Camera behavior will become controllable. The visual “tells” of generative systems will become less reliable. I do not think this leads to the end of truth. I think it leads to a change in trust architecture. The question will move from “Does this look real?” toward “What network of relations supports this claim?” Who published it? What provenance is available? Are there independent sources? Can the media be audited? What is the epistemic status of reconstruction? Does the institution have a reputation to lose? Is uncertainty represented honestly? This is why I prefer the phrase systemic trust to visual trust. Systemic trust is imperfect and political. Institutions can fail. Credentials can be misunderstood. Powerful actors can abuse standards. But it is more realistic than imagining that human eyes will continue to authenticate reality from pixel appearance alone.

I do not want to become an artist announcing the death of truth

The phrase “post-truth” can become a form of aesthetic fatalism. If everything can be fabricated, then nothing can be known. If nothing can be known, the artist is free to make anything. I reject that conclusion. When shared evidence becomes harder to establish, the responsibility to build better evidence becomes more important, not less. Art cannot replace journalism, courts, science, or investigation. But art can make the construction of trust perceptible. It can show how a framing decision changes evidence. It can expose the difference between a credential and a claim. It can preserve uncertainty instead of filling it. It can reveal how synthetic subjectivity makes us trust a speaker. It can show cultural recognizability functioning like false evidence. It can make an impossible camera position feel like a witness, then reveal that the witnessing position never existed. These are specifically cinematic problems.

WHO IS LOOKING? becomes a larger structure connecting my works

The three works I am developing now can be understood as three different crises of the witness. In Alien Friend, a nonhuman observer can record accurately and understand badly. In Does AI Know It Is AI?, a synthetic speaker can appear to possess an interior witness to its own states without that interiority being established. In Ethnic Dolls: YAO, a model can produce culturally recognizable bodies that appear to witness or represent a culture while actually reflecting probability, public imagery, and my own external selection. This is why I keep returning to the curatorial question WHO IS LOOKING? The question is no longer simply who stands behind the camera. It includes the system that classifies, the model that generates, the institution that authenticates, the spectator who attributes subjectivity, the archive that preserves, and the artist who chooses which uncertainty to expose. Authorship becomes part of witnessing. To make an image public is to place a claim into a field of possible belief.

My next formal method is evidentiary friction

I do not want the spectator to solve a puzzle and feel clever for detecting AI. I want something slower. I call it Evidentiary Friction. A work produces evidentiary friction when the spectator cannot consume credibility instantly. A convincing video lacks context. A rough video has strong provenance. A reconstruction is beautifully clear but openly uncertain. A real recording is badly framed. A synthetic speaker is emotionally persuasive but ontologically unresolved. A cultural image is recognizable but historically unstable. The spectator is forced to move among different forms of support rather than allowing visual polish to decide. This friction can be built through montage. Through sound. Through the timing of disclosure. Through multiple screens. Through captions that state uncertainty rather than eliminate it. Through provenance records that contradict initial impressions. The artistic goal is not suspicion for its own sake. It is to restore time to judgment.

The artist after the witness is not the owner of truth but the designer of accountable uncertainty

I used to think artistic responsibility meant being truthful about whether I used AI. I now think that is only the beginning. The deeper responsibility is designing the relation among image, claim, source, uncertainty, subject, and spectator. Sometimes I will create complete fiction. Sometimes I will use real photography. Sometimes I will reconstruct. Sometimes I will simulate. Sometimes I will deliberately combine these levels. What matters is that I know which operation is occurring and do not borrow an evidentiary authority the work has not earned. I call this accountable uncertainty. It does not ask art to become certain. It asks the artist to be precise about where certainty ends.

Final note: after the witness, cinema does not become less real; reality becomes a relation the film has to construct

The generated image does not end cinema’s relation to reality. It makes that relation less automatic. This is the difficult freedom of post-camera cinema. When the camera is no longer required as the prior witness, the filmmaker has to build other forms of accountability. Provenance. Context. Source relations. Embodied consequences. Cultural specificity. Uncertainty. Formal seams. Institutional responsibility. The absence of a prior scene does not free me from the world. It obliges me to describe more carefully how the world enters the image. That is the direction I want my practice to take. I do not want to produce images that simply look true. I want to produce works that understand the difference between looking true, coming from a traceable source, representing an event, asking for belief, and taking responsibility for the belief they produce. The future of moving-image art will not be decided by whether synthetic images can fool us perfectly. It will be decided by whether artists can build forms in which truth, fiction, speculation, evidence, and subjectivity remain distinguishable without becoming aesthetically dead. That is what I mean by the image after the witness. The witness has not vanished. Its position has been distributed. And the work now begins by asking who, or what, is able to answer for what the image claims to have seen.
