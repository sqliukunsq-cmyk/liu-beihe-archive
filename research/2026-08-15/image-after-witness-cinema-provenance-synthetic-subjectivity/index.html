The Image After the Witness: Cinema, Provenance, and Synthetic Subjectivity

The Image After the Witness: Cinema, Provenance, and Synthetic Subjectivity

作者 / Author：刘北河 LIU BEIHE 独立影像艺术家、导演 / Independent Moving-Image Artist & Filmmaker LIU BEIHE Research Archive / 刘北河研究档案

I no longer begin with the question “Is this image real?”

I begin with a harder question: what kind of claim is this image asking me to accept, and who is capable of answering for that claim? Generative moving images have made the old binary of real versus fake increasingly inadequate. A photograph can be captured from an event and still mislead through framing, omission, captioning, or editing. A generated image can depict an event that never occurred and still communicate a truthful metaphor. The ethical problem begins when these different claims are allowed to masquerade as one another. I call the present condition the image after the witness. This does not mean witnesses disappear. It means an image can now perform many of the visual gestures of witnessing without any camera having occupied the represented scene.

The index was never enough, but its fracture matters

Photography acquired evidentiary authority historically. The daguerreotype did not arrive with an automatic philosophical guarantee; institutions, archives, newspapers, families, and courts gradually stabilized conventions of trust. André Bazin’s ontology of the photographic image and Roland Barthes’s insistence on the photograph’s relation to a “that-has-been” belong to a world in which the physical encounter between light, object, and recording surface remained culturally central. Post-camera cinema does not simply abolish that history. It parasitizes it. A generated shot can inherit depth of field, lens behavior, handheld instability, documentary framing, and the emotional rhetoric of archival footage without inheriting the prior scene that gave those conventions their evidentiary force. This is why I use the phrase collapse of visual evidence carefully. Evidence has not disappeared. Rather, visual appearance alone is becoming a weaker basis for deciding what happened.

Provenance is necessary, but provenance is not truth

C2PA’s Content Credentials infrastructure is one important response. Version 2.4, published in April 2026, extends the specification with additional assertions and formats. Such systems can help establish a chain of provenance: how an asset was created, transformed, signed, or incorporated into another asset. I support this direction. But I refuse to treat provenance as a truth machine. A perfectly authenticated recording can still be propaganda. A signed file can still omit context. Provenance answers questions about the history of an asset; it does not automatically settle the truth of the world represented inside it. I therefore distinguish provenance truth from event truth. The first concerns the traceable history of the media object. The second concerns whether the claims we infer from it are justified. Future visual literacy will require both.

Synthetic subjectivity arrives before machine consciousness

My work Does AI Know It Is AI? grows from a related distinction. I am not trying to prove that a machine is conscious. I am interested in the fact that moving images can produce the appearance of interiority long before we have any reason to conclude that the generating system possesses an inner life. Cinema has always produced interiority through form. The Kuleshov effect demonstrates how spectators infer emotion through montage. Bresson could reduce expressive acting and still produce profound psychological intensity. Chris Marker could build memory from still photographs, voice, and temporal structure. The spectator is an active manufacturer of subjectivity. Generative systems intensify this condition because face, voice, gesture, environment, and dialogue can all be synthesized. I call the result synthetic subjectivity: a coherent impression that someone is there, remembering, desiring, or suffering, even when no continuous embodied performer occupies the represented event.

The strongest objection changes my argument

A strong objection is obvious: I never directly access another human being’s consciousness either. Human subjectivity is also inferred through behavior, language, and embodiment. Why should synthetic characters be treated differently? I accept the first half of the objection. I cannot prove another person’s consciousness from an image. But the difference I care about is not metaphysical certainty. It is the structure of consequence. A human performer can be exhausted, exploited, paid, injured, refuse consent, age, and die. A synthetic character does not occupy those institutional positions in the same way, although its production may depend on the data and identities of people who do. This moves the question from “Is the character really conscious?” to “Who bears the consequences of making this character appear conscious?”

Alien Friend and the failure of nonhuman seeing

In Alien Friend, I want to approach the problem from the opposite direction. Instead of making a synthetic entity seem more human, I want to construct a gaze that repeatedly fails to understand human significance. The alien may identify faces perfectly while misunderstanding mourning. It may model traffic accurately while failing to understand waiting. Its visual system may be technically competent and semantically lost. This is why I do not want the nonhuman gaze to be represented by a familiar science-fiction HUD. A heads-up display is already a human visual convention. I want difference to emerge through duration, framing, attention, and sound: a camera that remains with an empty doorway after the person has left; an acoustic hierarchy in which electrical hum matters more than dialogue; a composition that ignores the face and attends to the object beside it. I call this semantic-misalignment gaze. Recognition succeeds while meaning fails.

Ethnic Dolls: YAO and the danger of plausible culture

The same structure appears in Ethnic Dolls: YAO. Generative models can produce culturally coded imagery that looks immediately plausible. The danger is not only hallucination. It is high-probability plausibility: a culture reduced to the visual signs most legible to a model and to a global audience. Here I want to distinguish cultural representation from cultural recognizability. A highly recognizable image may be a poor representation precisely because it intensifies stereotypes. My working method is therefore to compare generations, trace recurring motifs, identify uncertain sources, and preserve disagreements rather than smoothing them into a single authoritative visual style.

Authorship becomes a chain of accountable decisions

The U.S. Copyright Office’s 2025 report on copyrightability and generative AI is useful to me not as a recipe for ownership but as a conceptual pressure. The Office emphasizes that prompts alone do not provide sufficient human authorship, while human selection, arrangement, modification, and perceptible expressive contribution may matter. This aligns with what I call negative craft. When generation is abundant, authorship increasingly appears in what I refuse: the beautiful shot that collapses cultural difference, the emotionally efficient voice that over-humanizes a machine, the seamless continuity that erases the instability central to a work. My production archive therefore needs to preserve not only outputs but rejected paths. A rejection ledger can record why a version was excluded. This is not bureaucratic self-defense. It is a method for discovering whether my aesthetic decisions actually form a coherent practice.

What I expect over the next decade

I expect provenance infrastructure to become ordinary. I expect synthetic performers to become more persistent and controllable. I expect video models to improve temporal coherence, audio generation, physical simulation, and reference control. None of these advances will solve the central artistic problem. The scarce resource will not be images. It will be accountable judgment. My next works must therefore make judgment perceptible without turning into lectures about technology. I want the spectator to feel the instability of evidence, the seduction of synthetic interiority, and the violence hidden inside effortless recognizability. The future of moving-image art will not be decided by whether AI can make cinema. It will be decided by whether artists can still make decisions that a system optimized for plausible completion would not make on its own.
