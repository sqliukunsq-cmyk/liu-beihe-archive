Post-Camera Cinema: The Broken Index and the Image Without a Prior Scene

Post-Camera Cinema: The Broken Index and the Image Without a Prior Scene

> Author：LIU BEIHE / 刘北河
Independent Moving-Image Artist & Filmmaker
LIU BEIHE Research Archive / 刘北河研究档案

I no longer think the most important thing about generative video is that it makes filmmaking cheaper

The first temptation of generative video is economic. A location that once required travel can be produced at a desk. A creature that once demanded prosthetics, VFX, or animation can be generated in minutes. A camera movement that would have required cranes, drones, motion control, or complex compositing can appear without any physical camera movement having taken place. All of this matters. Production structures will change because of it. But if generative moving images are important only because they allow us to imitate existing cinema more cheaply, then their artistic significance is limited. We would have found a new production method without finding a new condition of the image. The question that increasingly matters to me is more fundamental: What happens to cinema when a photographic-looking image no longer requires a prior scene that was photographed? I use the term Post-Camera Cinema as a working artistic framework for this condition. I do not mean that cameras are disappearing, nor that photography has become obsolete. I mean that the camera has lost its historical monopoly over the production of images that look as though they were captured from a world in front of a lens. A generated image may contain depth of field, motion blur, lens breathing, hand-held instability, skin texture, atmospheric perspective, camera movement, accidental-looking framing, and all the small imperfections through which we have learned to recognize photographic reality. Yet none of these visible traces necessarily point back to a corresponding photographic event. The image can look captured without having been captured. For me, this is the conceptual rupture.

Cinema has never been only photography, but photography gave cinema a hidden contract with the event

It would be historically naive to imagine that cinema was once pure photographic truth and has only now become synthetic. Méliès was already interrupting and transforming the photographed world at the end of the nineteenth century. Animation demonstrated that moving-image experience did not require photographed movement at all. Optical compositing, matte painting, stop motion, blue-screen processes, digital VFX, CGI, and virtual production have continuously separated what appears on screen from what physically existed at the moment of capture. So Post-Camera Cinema cannot simply mean “images of things that were never really there.” Cinema has always contained such images. What I am trying to describe is more specific. Even in a highly fictional live-action film, there has usually been a second reality underneath the fiction: an actor actually stood somewhere; light struck a sensor or film emulsion; a camera occupied a position; a body moved through time. The story could be invented, but the act of photographing left a trace of something that had physically occurred in front of an apparatus. That trace never guaranteed truth. A staged photograph can lie. A documentary image can be edited dishonestly. A camera can exclude more than it reveals. Yet the photographic process still linked the appearance of an image to a prior material encounter. William J. Mitchell’s The Reconfigured Eye, published in the early digital era, already described how digital manipulation destabilized the cultural authority of photographic truth. [1] Generative video pushes the problem further. We are no longer only modifying a trace. We can increasingly synthesize the visual appearance of a trace without a corresponding capture event. This is why I distinguish between two events: The photographic event — a relation between a physical scene, light, apparatus, and recording process. The image event — the appearance of an image to a viewer, institution, platform, archive, or system, where it becomes meaningful and operative. Historically these events were often connected. In Post-Camera Cinema, they can separate.

The index is not simply dead; it has become unreliable as a default assumption

The history of photography theory often returns to the index — the idea that the photograph bears a physical relation to what stood before the camera. I am wary of treating this relation as a guarantee of factual truth. The history of propaganda, forensic misuse, colonial archives, advertising, and staged photography makes such innocence impossible. But I also do not want to dismiss the index as irrelevant. The important change is not that photographic evidence was ever perfect. It is that the viewer has long been trained to make a weak but powerful assumption: if something looks photographically plausible, some version of that thing was probably once available to a recording apparatus. Generative images weaken that assumption. This is what I call the broken index. “Broken” does not mean completely destroyed. Cameras remain indexical in important ways. Captured images remain central to journalism, archives, personal memory, science, and art. But photographic appearance and capture history can no longer be casually treated as the same thing. C2PA’s Content Credentials are interesting to me precisely because they respond to this separation at the level of infrastructure. The standard records cryptographically verifiable provenance: information about origin, edits, tools, and asset history. [2] Yet C2PA is explicit that provenance alone does not determine whether an image is true or factual. [3] That distinction is essential. The image is increasingly unable to carry its evidentiary history entirely inside its visible surface. Trust begins to migrate outward — toward provenance, institutions, context, chain of custody, and corroboration. Post-Camera Cinema emerges inside this changing visual contract.

I am less interested in an image that looks impossible than in an ordinary image with an impossible origin

Much AI moving-image work has understandably been drawn toward spectacle: bodies transforming into architecture, impossible camera movements, dream environments, unstable faces, surreal continuous metamorphosis. I enjoy many of these possibilities, but they are not what most interests me artistically. An obviously impossible image announces itself as fiction. It asks the viewer to admire transformation. I am more interested in an ordinary image whose production history is impossible to infer from appearance alone. A badly framed phone video. A dim surveillance corridor. A banal interview. A family dinner. A person waiting for a bus. A camera that appears to lose focus at the wrong moment. These images draw on an enormous cultural memory of photographic contingency. We associate technical imperfection with the resistance of reality: the camera failed to control the event because the event existed independently of the camera. Generative systems can increasingly synthesize that contingency. This means that “accident” itself can become designed. For me, this is far more destabilizing than visual spectacle.

Generative video does not remove materiality; it relocates it

The phrase “camera-less” can create a misleading fantasy of dematerialization. A generated image is not immaterial. It depends on training data, data centers, energy, GPUs, software, model architectures, interface design, network infrastructure, file formats, and human labor. What disappears is not materiality but one specific material relation: the requirement that the depicted scene physically precede its photographic appearance. Vilém Flusser’s writing on technical images remains useful here. The technical image is not simply a transparent window onto the world. It is produced through apparatuses and programs that structure what can appear. [4] Generative systems intensify this condition. The apparatus becomes less visible while becoming more culturally expansive. A viewer sees a snow-covered street. The viewer does not see the model’s training distribution, the inference process, the server infrastructure, the prompt history, the rejected versions, or the human judgments that selected the final image. Post-Camera Cinema therefore demands a new kind of material awareness. The absence of a set does not mean the absence of conditions. It means that the conditions have moved elsewhere.

Sora made me think less about “AI filmmaking” and more about probabilistic worlds

OpenAI’s 2024 technical report on Sora described an approach in which images and videos are represented as spatiotemporal patches and modeled in a compressed latent space. The report presented scaling video generation as a possible path toward more general world simulators, while also documenting clear limitations in physical causality, object permanence, and long-term consistency. [5] I do not interpret this as evidence that a model “understands the world” in a human sense. What interests me is the shift in the director’s object of work. A camera confronts a world that already exists in front of it. A generative model operates within a learned space of visual possibility. Given conditions, it predicts plausible continuations. This transforms the filmmaker’s relationship to the image. I am no longer only arranging bodies, light, and space in front of a recording apparatus. I am also navigating a probability field of possible images. The problem becomes less: “How do I capture this?” And more: “Among all the worlds this system can propose, which one should exist in my film — and why?” This is where Post-Camera Cinema connects to my thinking about authorship. When possible images become abundant, the artistic problem moves from production scarcity toward judgment scarcity.

I do not want generative cinema to become an inexpensive imitation of expensive cinema

One future of generative moving images is obvious: cheaper advertising, cheaper previs, cheaper VFX, cheaper fantasy environments, cheaper extras, cheaper establishing shots. Industrially, that future is significant. Artistically, it is not enough for me. If every use of generation is evaluated by how successfully it imitates an ARRI camera, a Hollywood lighting setup, a luxury commercial, or an already-known cinematic aesthetic, then the technology becomes conservative precisely when it appears most radical. It reproduces the old image more efficiently. The question I keep asking is: What image becomes meaningful specifically because there was no prior photographic scene? A room whose architecture changes continuously without a cut. A character whose childhood memory and present body occupy the same apparently continuous shot. A landscape that retains photographic continuity while its historical period slowly changes. A face that remains emotionally coherent while its identity history becomes unstable. These effects could theoretically be built with conventional VFX. That is not the point. The distinctive condition of generative imagery is that instability can become the default state of the world rather than an exceptional effect applied to an otherwise stable shot. I am interested in using that instability structurally.

“Cinema Without a Set” is not a fantasy of total control; it is a test of what happens when reality stops resisting me

A physical set resists a director. An actor becomes tired. Weather changes. A location limits camera placement. Light disappears. A body cannot execute an impossible motion indefinitely. These constraints are frustrating, but they are also ethically and aesthetically productive. Reality pushes back. Generative systems can create the opposite sensation: the world appears infinitely obedient. Ask for rain, and rain appears. Ask for grief, and a face performs grief. Ask for ruins, and a catastrophe becomes an aesthetic option. This obedience makes me uneasy. I have started to think that Post-Camera Cinema needs an ethics of self-imposed resistance. If the world no longer refuses me, I have to construct refusal inside my method. What should I not generate? Which body should not become an interchangeable visual asset? When should I return to live action because the presence of another person must resist my control? When does beauty become an ethical failure? When should technical imperfection remain because it reveals the limits of the system? This is where my idea of Negative Craft becomes relevant. In an environment of abundant possibility, refusal becomes part of form.

The generated performer changes what I mean by performance

A particularly difficult problem concerns the body. Traditional screen performance is recorded through duration. The actor’s body carries fatigue, breath, hesitation, muscle tension, memory, and the irreducible unpredictability of another person. A synthetic performer can produce visible signs of all these things without the corresponding embodied history. This does not mean synthetic performance is artistically empty. Animation has always created powerful characters without a photographed human performance directly matching every visible movement. But the ontological relation changes. I have to distinguish between recording a performance and constructing the effects by which performance is perceived. This is central to Does AI Know It Is AI?. A synthetic figure may pause, breathe, remember, hesitate, and look emotionally vulnerable. Yet those signs do not emerge from a life that preceded the image. Post-Camera Cinema therefore makes acting inseparable from my concept of Synthetic Subjectivity: the ways an image can produce the feeling of an interior subject even when no corresponding photographed subject occupied the scene.

I do not think cameras will become less important; I think their importance will become more deliberate

“Post-camera” can sound triumphalist, as if the camera belongs to an obsolete era. I mean the opposite. As synthetic images become easier to produce, live capture may acquire new value precisely because it is no longer technologically necessary for every image. A real body unfolding in real time can become an artistic choice rather than a default production requirement. A real weather system, an uncontrolled street, a nonrepeatable performance, the resistance of an actual location — these may become more meaningful when they are placed beside images that could have been entirely synthesized. I suspect mature future moving-image practices will not divide neatly into “AI” and “non-AI.” The more interesting distinction will be why each image requires the material process that produced it. This shot must be captured because I need reality to resist me. That shot must be generated because I need a continuous world that has no photographic subject. This archive must retain original provenance because its evidentiary relation matters. This voice must be synthetic because the work concerns a subject whose body is itself an interface. Media choice becomes part of authorship.

The strongest objection to Post-Camera Cinema is that animation and CGI already did all of this

I take this objection seriously. Animation has never required a photographed scene. CGI has produced complete virtual worlds for decades. Matte painting and compositing have long broken the unity of the filmed set. So why name a new condition? My answer is not that generative systems are technologically “more advanced.” The distinction I am trying to isolate lies in their relationship to photographic appearance and probabilistic production. Animation conventionally declares its constructedness. CGI traditionally requires explicit modeling, rigging, animation, lighting, simulation, and rendering to build a world. Generative video learns from vast fields of prior visual culture and can directly produce imagery whose default aspiration is often photographic plausibility. The paradox is therefore specific: The image has no photographic scene, yet it inherits photography’s visual grammar. It is photography-like without having been photographed. This “photographicity without photography” is what makes Post-Camera Cinema useful to me as a concept.

Post-Camera Cinema is becoming the connective tissue between my works

I did not begin with a theory and then design works to illustrate it. The theory is emerging retrospectively from the problems the works keep forcing me to confront. Alien Friend asks what happens when the owner of the camera position becomes uncertain. Its generative passages allow me to build a continuous visual experience that does not need to belong to a physical camera subject. Does AI Know It Is AI? asks what happens when documentary-like signs of embodiment and testimony can be synthesized without a photographed person having lived the memories being described. Ethnic Dolls: YAO asks what happens when cultural identity is not photographed from a particular individual but statistically reconstructed from a learned field of cultural images. These are different works, but each breaks a different link in the traditional chain between scene, subject, camera, and image. This is why “AI film” feels too narrow as a long-term description of my practice. AI is a technology class. Post-Camera Cinema is a condition of the image.

The question of truth does not disappear; it becomes infrastructural

My idea of the Collapse of Visual Evidence develops directly from Post-Camera Cinema. If photographic appearance no longer guarantees a capture event, visual trust shifts toward provenance, context, institutional credibility, and corroboration. C2PA is one attempt to build infrastructure for this new condition. NIST has likewise documented a range of approaches to synthetic-content transparency, including provenance, watermarking, and detection. [6] The EU AI Act’s Article 50, with transparency obligations applying from August 2026 for certain AI-generated or manipulated content, shows that the synthetic origin of an image is becoming a matter of public policy rather than merely a production detail. [7] For an artist, however, transparency is not only compliance. It can become form. A generated image can carry its production history as part of the work. A fictional archive can openly disclose that its events never happened while still generating an intense sense of evidence. A film can alternate between captured and generated material without using either as a moral shorthand for “true” and “false.” The richer artistic question is not whether the image is synthetic. It is what relation the image asks us to establish with reality.

I think the director’s role may shift from controlling scenes to composing incompatible visual regimes

The classic image of the director is someone who organizes a scene: actor, camera, light, movement, duration. In a Post-Camera environment, I increasingly imagine the director as someone who composes different regimes of visibility. A photographed body. A generated environment. A machine-vision classification. An archival trace. A synthetic voice. A real-time sensor. A platform-generated recommendation. Each has a different relation to reality, authorship, and evidence. The director’s task may become less about making them look seamless and more about deciding when their differences should remain visible. I am not interested in a future cinema where every medium disappears into one frictionless synthetic image. Friction is where meaning appears.

Over the next decade, I want to ask not whether cameras survive, but when a scene must actually happen

I am certain cameras will survive. What changes is that their necessity can no longer be assumed. This creates a harder artistic question than technological replacement: When must a person really stand there? When must weather really happen? When must a location resist me? When must an image carry a trace of an event? And conversely: When does a work require a world that never existed as a set? When does the absence of a prior scene become the meaning of the image rather than a production shortcut? This is how I now understand Post-Camera Cinema. It is not cinema “after” the camera in chronological terms. It is cinema after the camera loses its monopoly over photographic appearance. Once that monopoly is gone, every image has to justify its material condition. For me, that does not make cinema less real. It makes the question of reality impossible to avoid.
