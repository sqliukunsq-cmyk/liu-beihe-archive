After the Prompt: Judgment, Provenance, and the Artist’s Right to Refuse

After the Prompt: Judgment, Provenance, and the Artist’s Right to Refuse

Author: LIU BEIHE / 刘北河 Independent Moving-Image Artist & Filmmaker LIU BEIHE Research Archive / 刘北河研究档案

I am less interested in prompting than in deciding

The public language around generative moving images still gives too much symbolic weight to the prompt. I understand why: the prompt is visible, quotable, and easy to imagine as a new kind of script. But in my own practice, the decisive artistic work begins after the first plausible image appears. It begins when I have to decide which possibility deserves duration, which face should not become a character, which beautiful shot is conceptually dishonest, and which technical failure reveals more than a polished result. The U.S. Copyright Office’s Part 2 report on AI and copyright is useful here not because copyright law can define art, but because it makes an important distinction. Mere prompting does not automatically supply the human authorship required for copyright, while human selection, arrangement, modification, and perceptible expressive contribution may matter. I take this not as a formula for authorship but as a pressure test: if I cannot explain my contribution beyond the initial instruction, perhaps I have not yet directed the work.

Provenance is not truth

C2PA 2.4, released in April 2026, expands the technical vocabulary of Content Credentials with crJSON, repository receipts, and further work on durable credentials. This matters, but I resist the fantasy that provenance solves truth. A provenance record can tell me something about an asset’s history; it cannot prove that the depicted event occurred. I therefore distinguish provenance chains from responsibility chains. The first describes what happened to the file. The second describes why I allowed the file to become part of the work. In a future version of Does AI Know It Is AI?, I want these two chains to diverge visibly. A synthetic speaker may deliver a perfectly coherent statement about its own condition. Beside it, a second layer can expose the production history: generated, rejected, regenerated, voice replaced, duration shortened, contextual claim removed. The work would not ask the audience to admire transparency. It would ask whether authorship becomes more legible when decisions are allowed to leave traces.

Labor has entered the image

The 2026 WGA MBA and SAG-AFTRA TV/Theatrical Agreement make this problem materially concrete. The WGA agreement requires written notice when companies license writers’ work to train a commercial generative AI system and allows the Guild to request discussion about the license, including remuneration. SAG-AFTRA’s 2026 agreement strengthens protections around digital replicas and synthetics, including bargaining requirements and a stated principle strongly favoring human performance. These are not peripheral industrial developments. They tell me that the synthetic image contains labor relations even when no worker is visible in the frame. A generated face can carry questions of consent; a model can carry questions of training; a synthetic performance can carry questions about what kind of human performance it displaces.

Refusal as form

I have been developing what I call negative craft: the artistic practice of producing meaning through refusal, deletion, constraint, delay, and non-use. Generative systems make this more urgent because abundance changes the psychology of editing. When every prompt yields several usable possibilities, the temptation is not scarcity but accumulation. I now want to move from refusal as workflow to refusal as form. In an installation, rejected images could remain visible as a peripheral archive. Their presence would not function as behind-the-scenes entertainment. Each rejected image would be accompanied by a reason: too culturally generic; too emotionally manipulative; too dependent on photographic realism; too eager to anthropomorphize the machine; too beautiful for the question the work is trying to ask.

Alien Friend and the rhythm of misunderstanding

This method changes Alien Friend. I do not want to design an alien face. I want to design a nonhuman system of interpretation. The character may identify a cup correctly but misunderstand the social meaning of offering water. It may detect two bodies in an embrace but classify the event as mutual occlusion. It may recognize tears but fail to infer grief. I call the temporal structure of these scenes the rhythm of misunderstanding. The audience should understand the human meaning first; the alien interpretation should arrive a moment later. That delay creates the work. It turns cognition into montage. The strongest objection is obvious: my “nonhuman” gaze is still authored by a human. I cannot escape anthropocentrism simply by drawing bounding boxes over an image. I accept this. The project does not claim to represent actual alien consciousness. It stages the limits of my own assumption that human meaning is the default grammar of reality.

Ethnic Dolls: YAO and the map of absence

A related problem appears in Ethnic Dolls: YAO. If I only catalogue stereotypical symbols repeatedly produced by generative models, I risk making a predictable critique of bias. I am now more interested in what the models repeatedly omit. I call this a map of cultural absence. A model may repeatedly produce ceremonial clothing, silver ornament, mountain landscapes, and posed bodies while rarely producing smartphones, rental apartments, factories, schools, migration, ordinary T-shirts, plastic stools, or hybrid urban identities. The absence is not proof of what a culture “really is.” It is evidence of what the model finds visually legible as culture. This is where Stuart Hall’s work on representation, Edward Said’s critique of Orientalism, visual anthropology, dataset documentation, and museum classification histories become practical rather than decorative references. They help me ask how visibility is produced, not simply whether an image is accurate.

What I expect from the next decade

Over the next three to ten years, generated moving images will become smoother, cheaper, and less visibly artificial. Technical failure will become a weaker source of critical distance. Provenance infrastructure will improve, labor agreements will continue to evolve, and synthetic performers will become increasingly negotiable assets. For me, this makes judgment more important, not less. The artist’s scarce resource will not be access to images. It will be the capacity to establish a reasoned boundary between what can be generated and what should enter the work. I therefore do not define my practice by whether I use AI. I define it by the conditions under which I permit an image to survive.
