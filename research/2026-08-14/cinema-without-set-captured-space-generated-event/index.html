Cinema Without a Set: From Captured Space to Generated Event

Cinema Without a Set: From Captured Space to Generated Event

作者 / Author：刘北河 LIU BEIHE
独立影像艺术家、导演 / Independent Moving-Image Artist & Filmmaker
LIU BEIHE Research Archive / 刘北河研究档案

I began to understand the set only when I could make an image without one

For most of my working life, a film image implied a prior negotiation with space. A wall existed before the camera arrived. A performer had to cross a measurable distance. Light came from somewhere, even when that “somewhere” was carefully fabricated. The lens occupied a physical position and therefore surrendered every other position. Production was full of resistance: weather, architecture, bodies, schedules, gravity, money. Generative moving image changes this relation in a way that is easy to underestimate. It does not merely give me a cheaper virtual set. It gives me an image of space that may have no complete spatial world behind it at all. A room can exist convincingly for five seconds without possessing a stable floor plan. A camera can appear to move through matter without any device traversing a volume. A body can turn and discover that the geometry behind it has been recomputed. I call this emerging condition Cinema Without a Set. By “set” I do not simply mean a constructed studio environment. I mean the prior spatial situation to which an image once had to answer. This does not announce the death of location shooting. I still consider the physical set one of cinema’s great machines of contingency. My point is narrower and more radical: the moving image no longer needs a prior scene in order to produce the appearance of one.

Post-camera cinema is not anti-camera cinema

I have been using the term Post-Camera Cinema to describe images whose existence is no longer guaranteed by a prior optical event. The phrase can sound triumphalist, as if the camera were an obsolete instrument. I reject that reading. The camera remains valuable precisely because it places the filmmaker in a causal relation with a world that can resist. Joanna Zylinska’s Nonhuman Photography helps widen the historical frame: CCTV, drones, satellite systems and medical imaging already weakened the assumption that photography is simply a human eye extended by a camera. Her later The Perception Machine follows computation further into the organization of contemporary seeing.[1] [2] Generative image systems introduce a different discontinuity. They do not only automate seeing. They can synthesize an appearance without receiving light from the depicted scene. That distinction changes directing. On a set, I direct bodies and devices in a shared physical situation. In a generative workflow, I increasingly direct conditions: reference images, prompts, masks, trajectories, duration, model choice, seeds, negative constraints, selection criteria, continuity rules. I am no longer only asking, “Where should the camera be?” I am asking, “What kind of world must remain stable for this shot to mean what I need it to mean?” I call this conditional directing.

A generated room is not necessarily a room

The classical illusion of cinema has always been partial. Studio streets have false backs. Matte paintings extend architecture. Rear projection places actors inside spaces they never entered. Montage connects rooms that may be continents apart. So what is genuinely new? The difference is not that generative cinema is fake while classical cinema was real. The difference is the ontological status of the off-screen world. A constructed set is incomplete, but its incomplete parts are physically determinate. A wall that ends two meters beyond frame still ends somewhere. In a generated shot, the unseen wall may have no determined state until another frame requires one. Space is not simply hidden; it may be uncomputed. This leads me to the concept of the local world: a visual world that is sufficiently coherent within the conditions of a shot but is not obligated to exist as a complete environment outside those conditions. Current technical research treats this as a problem to solve. Benchmarks for video generation increasingly measure object permanence, relation stability, causal consistency, flicker and physical plausibility. Work such as World Consistency Score, PhyWorld and reference-free physical consistency assessment attempts to make generated video behave more like a stable world.[3] [4] [5] From an engineering perspective, I understand why. A world model intended for robotics cannot casually move a table between frames. But an artwork is not a robotics benchmark. My question is whether some forms of spatial instability can become intentional language rather than defects.

Alien Friend: the nonhuman gaze may begin with spatial continuity

In Alien Friend, I do not want the alien perspective to be announced by a green tint, a fisheye lens, heat vision or a scanning interface. Those signs have become shortcuts. They tell the audience, “This is nonhuman,” while leaving the deeper grammar of cinematic attention unchanged. I am more interested in making the world itself obey a slightly different continuity regime. Imagine a character walking through a domestic room. The light remains consistent. The furniture remains recognizable. The sound remains continuous. Yet each time the shot changes, distances drift by a few percent. A doorway is subtly too far from a window. A table has migrated without movement. The viewer cannot immediately identify an error, but bodily confidence in the room begins to weaken. I call this ontological drift when the instability is deliberately organized around the work’s concept rather than accepted as random model failure. The alien quality does not come from what the image depicts. It comes from what the image considers necessary to preserve. Human spatial perception depends on strong assumptions of object permanence and stable geometry. What if a fictional nonhuman observer preserved texture, rhythm or temperature more faithfully than distance? The film could remain visually calm while its world quietly refuses human spatial priorities. This is more interesting to me than spectacle.

Harun Farocki: an image can be part of an operation

Harun Farocki’s Eye / Machine III remains one of the clearest precedents for thinking beyond images as representations. His “operational images” do not merely depict a process; they participate in one. Guidance systems compare, classify and act. The image is embedded in decision.[6] Generative world models push this lineage into simulation. A generated video can increasingly function not only as something to watch, but as a provisional environment in which a system predicts what could happen next. This matters for cinema because it changes the meaning of a shot. A shot has historically been evidence of a recording procedure, however manipulated. A generated shot may instead be the visible surface of a hypothesis. It can say: not “this was there,” but “under these conditions, this is one plausible event.” That is a profound shift from captured space to generated event.

The strongest objection: a cinema without a set risks becoming a cinema without an other

I take this objection seriously. The physical world does more than provide texture. It confronts the director with other agents. Actors disagree. Locations refuse access. Weather destroys plans. Materials behave badly. Accidents reshape rhythm. The set is not merely an expensive obstacle; it is an ethical situation in which my intention is not sovereign. A fully generative workflow can remove much of this resistance. Characters do not need to object. Weather can be regenerated. Architecture can be changed without negotiation. The director risks becoming a ruler of obedient images. This is why I refuse the simple story that generative cinema “liberates imagination.” Imagination without resistance can become visually rich and existentially poor. For me, the answer is not to return nostalgically to a pre-generative cinema. It is to design new forms of resistance. One method is hybrid production: combine captured material with generated material so that each exposes the other’s assumptions. Another is negative craft: deliberately restrict generation, delete easy beauty, preserve ambiguity, and define stopping conditions before infinite variation replaces judgment. A third is ethical resistance: determine what cannot be generated because a person, culture or context should not become frictionless material.

Ethnic Dolls: YAO and the danger of a frictionless cultural set

This concern becomes acute in Ethnic Dolls: YAO. A generative system can quickly build an apparently coherent “Yao world” from recurring visual cues: clothing, silver ornaments, textiles, facial types, landscapes. The result may be beautiful and still be epistemically weak. It can transform culture into a decorative environment whose coherence comes from statistical repetition rather than lived relation. UNESCO’s recent work on AI and culture has placed cultural sovereignty, algorithmic homogenization, creators’ rights and linguistic/cultural diversity at the center of governance discussions.[7] [8] For my work, this means a generated cultural space must never be treated as a neutral set. I need to know which references come from where, what historical differences are being compressed, which visual signs the model overvalues, and where my own desire for coherence is producing a stereotype. Sometimes the most responsible aesthetic decision may be to leave the world incomplete.

The viewing site becomes more important when the shooting site disappears

There is another paradox. If the production image can lose its physical set, the exhibition or screening space becomes more important. The viewer still has a body. Projection size, speaker placement, seat distance, darkness, screen architecture and duration remain stubbornly physical. I am interested in making this contradiction explicit. A generated camera might pass through a wall, while the installation forces the viewer to walk around a real wall. A synthetic character may have no physical body, but its face may be projected at a scale that dominates the viewer. A digitally impossible room may be accompanied by sound sources whose locations are materially precise. In such a work, cinema would have no original shooting set but a very strong viewing set. This may be where post-camera cinema regains embodiment.

Transparency changes spatial authorship too

The European Union’s Article 50 AI transparency obligations began applying on 2 August 2026, with requirements concerning machine-readable marking and disclosure in specified cases of AI-generated or manipulated content, while preserving an adapted approach for evidently artistic and fictional work.[9] C2PA’s evolving Content Credentials standard similarly attempts to preserve provenance information across digital media.[10] These systems may seem administrative, but they influence aesthetics. Once a cinematic space has no prior physical set, metadata and provenance become part of the work’s relation to reality. The audience may need to know not where the camera stood, but what conditions produced the apparent space. I do not want to cover an artwork in compliance labels. I do want to maintain an authorial record: what was captured, what was generated, what real identities entered the process, what model stages mattered, and where a fictional world deliberately imitates documentary grammar. The disappearance of the set should not mean the disappearance of responsibility.

The second death of the director would be automatic conditionality

Generative cinema does not automatically strengthen directing. It can weaken it. If platforms increasingly automate camera movement, continuity, performance, lighting and style, the director may become someone who merely requests a scene and chooses among polished results. That would be what I call the second death of the director: not the historical theoretical “death” of auteur authority, but the practical outsourcing of decisions to optimization systems. Conditional directing is my attempt to resist that outcome. The director must define what can vary and what cannot. Which inconsistency is expressive? Which continuity is essential? Which visual cue is culturally dangerous? When must a model stop? What kind of camera movement would be physically impossible, and why should the work use it? Which generated detail should be deleted precisely because it is too persuasive? Directing becomes less about commanding a set and more about designing a field of permissible events.

I do not want a world that obeys me too easily

Over the next three to ten years, generative video will almost certainly become longer, more controllable and more physically coherent. The engineering problem of stable worlds will improve. That will make the artistic problem harder. Today, a model’s instability can accidentally generate strangeness. Tomorrow, strangeness will need to be authored. I want to preserve the most valuable function of the set even when no set exists: resistance. Sometimes that resistance will come from real bodies. Sometimes from cultural limits. Sometimes from deliberately constrained generation. Sometimes from sound, duration or installation architecture. Sometimes from an image that refuses to become fully coherent. Cinema without a set should not mean cinema without the world. For me, the future of post-camera cinema depends on whether I can create images that are free from the necessity of a prior scene without becoming free from responsibility, history, bodies and consequence.

The camera used to certify a position in the world

There is an ethical dimension to the camera position that becomes clearer when the physical camera disappears. In documentary practice, the question “where were you standing?” is never purely technical. Distance can express access, permission, danger, intimacy and power. A close shot may imply that the filmmaker was allowed to approach. A high angle may reveal a privileged location. A hidden camera may raise questions of consent. Even fictional cinema inherits some of this relation because the lens still occupies a finite place among bodies and objects. A generated camera position can be radically different. It may exist inside a wall, above an event no aircraft ever crossed, inside a historical room where no camera was present, or beside a person who never performed the depicted action. It is capable of producing the visual grammar of witnessing without the social and physical conditions that traditionally made witnessing possible. I call this the disembodied camera position. I do not use the phrase to claim that generated images have no material infrastructure. They obviously depend on servers, interfaces, datasets, electricity, labor and human decisions. “Disembodied” describes the relation between the apparent viewpoint and the depicted world. The viewpoint does not have to correspond to a body or device that could have occupied that place. For artistic cinema, this freedom can be extraordinary. It can allow impossible spatial relations, nonhuman movement and images that no practical rig could produce. But freedom from the physical position should not become freedom from ethical position. If I generate a close-range view of a historical atrocity, the fact that the shot is technically possible does not answer whether I should produce it or what authority that view claims. This is where Cinema Without a Set intersects with another concept I am developing, Synthetic Witness. A generated image can look as though something witnessed an event even when nothing did. The cinematic question is no longer only “what does the shot show?” It is also “what kind of witness does the shot pretend to be?”

A world model and a film set have different obligations

Technical research in video world models often evaluates whether an environment remains stable enough to support prediction, robotics or embodied agents. Those systems need strong object permanence and causal consistency. If a robot reaches for a cup, the cup cannot simply migrate across the table because the model has forgotten its location. In that context, physical fidelity is not an aesthetic preference; it is operational necessity. Cinema does not have the same obligation. A film can violate geometry, time and causality and still be coherent at another level. Surrealist cinema, montage, dream sequences, essay film and installation have always reorganized spatial logic. The fact that generative systems are being trained to become more physically faithful does not mean artistic moving image should simply imitate the benchmark definition of a successful world. This distinction matters because generative artists can easily inherit engineering goals without noticing. “Better consistency” sounds universally desirable. Sometimes it is. Character continuity may be essential to a narrative. A stable room may be necessary for a specific tension. But if I automatically equate technical consistency with artistic quality, I allow the model’s optimization target to become my aesthetic target. My task is to decide which consistencies the work needs. A film may need emotional continuity but not architectural continuity. It may need acoustic continuity while allowing visual drift. It may need a face to remain stable while personality changes. It may need a location to remain visually inconsistent because the work concerns memory rather than measurement. This is a major part of conditional directing. I am not merely asking the system for more control. I am deciding what control should serve.

From continuity error to authored discontinuity

Current generative video still produces many discontinuities accidentally. Objects disappear. Clothing changes. architecture mutates. Motion becomes physically implausible. It would be intellectually lazy to declare every such failure “experimental cinema.” Random failure is not automatically artistic resistance. I therefore distinguish system discontinuity from authored discontinuity. System discontinuity is an unwanted break produced by instability in the model. Authored discontinuity is a break that has been deliberately incorporated into the work’s structure and can be explained in relation to its conceptual problem. The difference is not necessarily visible in a single frame. It appears through method. Suppose a room changes scale between shots. If the change happens unpredictably and undermines the scene, I treat it as failure. If I establish a rule in Alien Friend that human faces remain geometrically stable but the surrounding architecture shifts whenever the alien attention changes, the same type of visual instability becomes part of a designed perceptual system. This is what I call ontological drift. The world is not simply “glitching.” Its ontology is being distributed according to another logic of attention. The discipline lies in repetition. A meaningful drift should develop a grammar. It should not be an excuse for whatever the model happens to do.

The set historically forced cinema to negotiate with matter

One reason I resist triumphalist narratives about fully generative production is that matter has often improved films by refusing intention. A location can be too small for the planned lens. An actor’s body can make a storyboard movement impossible. A practical object can sound wrong. Rain can destroy continuity. A wall can block the ideal camera path. These events are frustrating, but they also force the director to revise imagination through contact with the world. The problem with an infinitely editable generated environment is not simply that it is artificial. The problem is that it may obey too well. Obedience can impoverish directing. If every wall moves when I ask, every actor changes emotion instantly, every sunset lasts forever and every lens position becomes available, the world stops arguing with me. Cinema risks becoming a closed loop between my preference and a system optimized to satisfy preference. This is why I think a mature generative practice needs constructed resistance. I have been developing the term Negative Craft for this broader method: deliberately limiting generation, setting stopping conditions, deleting easy beauty, preserving unresolved gaps and refusing certain possibilities even when technically available. Negative craft is not nostalgia for expensive production. It is an attempt to restore the function that material resistance once performed: forcing me to decide.

A generated set can still have rules strong enough to resist me

The solution is not necessarily to return to physical constraints. Computational environments can also be designed to resist. Before generating a sequence, I can establish rules that I am not allowed to violate simply because a later version looks better. A room may have a fixed acoustic identity even if its geometry changes. A character may never receive a conventional close-up. A particular object may disappear only after no human is looking at it. Camera motion may never accelerate beyond a defined threshold. Generated cultural imagery may require source documentation before entering the work. These rules create a conditional set. The set is no longer only architecture. It is a system of permissions and prohibitions. This changes production design. A production designer in a generative workflow may increasingly work with constraints, references, spatial invariants, model behaviors and provenance, not just visible surfaces. Cinematography may involve deciding which kinds of impossible camera behavior are meaningful rather than merely spectacular. Editing may involve comparing worlds that were generated under different rule systems rather than simply choosing takes. I do not know what future job titles will become, and I am not interested in predicting an industry org chart. What matters for my practice is that the “set” becomes a conceptual framework for what the image is allowed to become.

The danger of frictionless cultural space

The ethical stakes become particularly clear in Ethnic Dolls: YAO. Generative systems can produce culturally recognizable spaces with remarkable speed. Given enough visual prompts, a model can synthesize textiles, architecture, clothing, landscapes and faces into a coherent environment that reads as a particular ethnic or regional identity. Coherence is exactly what worries me. Cultures are not coherent image libraries. They contain internal differences, migrations, historical changes, contested meanings, regional variation and material practices that cannot be reduced to a stable style. A frictionless generated “Yao world” could become more visually convincing precisely as it becomes less historically specific. This creates what I would call cultural set compression: a model turns a heterogeneous culture into a production-design package. UNESCO’s recent work on AI and culture is useful here because it moves the debate beyond copyright toward cultural sovereignty, algorithmic homogenization, language diversity and creators’ rights. Those concerns remind me that a generated set is not simply an empty container. The visual sources from which it is assembled have histories and owners. For Ethnic Dolls: YAO, I therefore need a different form of conditional directing. Some references may require provenance. Some motifs should not be combined merely because the model finds them statistically compatible. Some materials may need consultation or should remain outside generation. Some differences should remain unresolved rather than merged into a coherent aesthetic identity. A responsible generated world may need to be less seamless.

The local world can become an installation strategy

The concept of the local world does not have to remain inside a screen. I am increasingly interested in translating it into installation. Imagine a projected room that appears stable only from one viewing position. As the viewer walks, different screens or projections reveal incompatible continuities. A door visible on one wall has no corresponding location on another. Sound remains continuous across the contradictions, giving the body a stronger sense of world than the image provides. The installation would not attempt to reproduce VR. I am less interested in total immersion than in partial worldhood: the sensation that a world exists just enough to support attention but not enough to become fully mapable. This could be particularly powerful for Alien Friend. The viewer might slowly understand that the apparent environment preserves a different set of invariants. Temperature-related sound, surface texture or rhythmic repetition could remain stable while Euclidean distance changes. The work would not explain this as “alien perception.” The viewer would discover the rule through bodily frustration. Here, the physical gallery becomes a counterweight to the generated image. The generated world is flexible; the wall the viewer must walk around is not. I call this distinction production site versus viewing site. As the production site becomes increasingly virtual or computational, the viewing site can regain material importance.

Sound can restore consequence to a visually unstable world

I have also begun to think that sound may be the strongest tool for making post-camera space feel consequential. Visual generation can change architecture from shot to shot, but a carefully designed sound field can maintain a stable sense of distance, enclosure and direction. The contradiction between unstable image and stable sound can make the viewer notice that spatial certainty is being constructed across modalities rather than given by the image alone. The reverse is also possible. The image can remain stable while sound changes spatial logic. A door remains visually in front of the character, but its sound begins behind the audience. A voice appears to move closer while the body recedes on screen. A generated camera passes through a wall, but the acoustic occlusion remains physically realistic. These are not effects for their own sake. They allow me to ask which sense the viewer trusts when a world disagrees with itself. This is one reason I do not want post-camera cinema to become synonymous with visual generation. A moving-image work is an orchestration of duration, sound, space, bodies, text, projection and audience position. The more generative tools make visual spectacle easy, the more important these other dimensions become.

The second death of the director is not replacement by AI; it is surrender of conditions

I use the phrase The Second Death of the Director carefully. I am not predicting that directors will disappear as jobs. Nor am I repeating twentieth-century debates about the “death of the author.” The second death I fear is practical: the director remains credited while more and more meaningful decisions are silently delegated to optimization systems. A platform may choose a conventional lens language, smooth continuity, emotionally legible performance, flattering light, rhythmically satisfying cuts and culturally familiar design because those outputs are statistically preferred. The filmmaker can still select from the results and therefore feel fully in control. But selection from pre-shaped possibilities is not the same as directing the conditions that created those possibilities. Conditional directing is my response. I need to know which decisions I am delegating and why. If I use an automated camera move, what visual assumption does it carry? If a model stabilizes a face, what kind of identity continuity does it privilege? If a system rewrites a prompt for safety or preference, how does that change the character? If a generated room becomes more beautiful after a refinement step, what cultural and architectural averages entered that beauty? Directing means making these conditions visible enough to judge them.

Provenance is becoming part of mise-en-scène

The new transparency requirements around AI-generated and manipulated content might appear separate from cinema aesthetics, but I believe they will gradually enter mise-en-scène itself. In photographic cinema, production documentation and the image were relatively separable. The viewer did not need to know the camera serial number to understand a shot. In synthetic media, provenance may increasingly shape how the image is interpreted. A generated historical scene, a digital replica of a performer and a fully fictional synthetic body may look equally convincing but carry radically different ethical relations. C2PA’s Content Credentials framework is one technical attempt to preserve information about media provenance and processing. The European Union’s Article 50 rules create legal transparency obligations in specified circumstances. Neither system tells an artist what a work should mean. But together they signal a broader cultural change: the conditions of image production are becoming part of the conditions of image trust. I want my practice to respond before this becomes a bureaucratic afterthought. For major works, I intend to maintain an authorial provenance record: which elements were captured; which were generated; whether identifiable real people were involved; what major model stages shaped the output; which cultural materials required contextual care; and which discontinuities were intentional rather than accidental. I do not intend to show all this information inside every screening. The archive and the work can remain different layers. But the information should exist.

The future set may be an argument rather than a place

The more I work with this idea, the less I think Cinema Without a Set is really about virtual production. It is about a shift in what a cinematic environment is. A set once organized bodies in space. A generated set can organize possibilities. It can determine which kinds of movement are allowed, which identities remain stable, which objects have permanence, which historical references can enter, which camera positions are possible, how much physics matters, how much uncertainty remains visible, and what the viewer is permitted to know about provenance. In that sense, the set becomes an argument about reality. A conventional set says: here is the world we will photograph. A conditional set says: here are the rules under which a world may temporarily appear. That difference may become one of the defining aesthetic problems of generative moving image.

What I want to test next

I do not want this theory to remain a vocabulary exercise. The next stage needs concrete production tests. For Alien Friend, I want to construct three versions of the same short spatial sequence. The first version will obey conventional continuity: stable architecture, physically plausible camera movement and human-centered attention. The second will keep architecture stable but introduce nonhuman salience: the camera will refuse human dramatic priorities while sound tracks micro-events that the characters ignore. The third will introduce controlled ontological drift: geometry will change according to a rule linked to the alien’s attention, while sound and material texture remain consistent. I want to compare not which version is “stranger,” but which version produces the strongest sense that the world is being organized by another subject. A second test will be installation-based. I want a generated image to perform an impossible passage through architecture while the audience physically encounters an obstacle in the gallery. The screen will offer frictionless movement; the body will meet resistance. A third test will relate to Ethnic Dolls: YAO. I want to construct a small generated environment using rigorously documented references, then deliberately remove the easiest culturally recognizable cues. If the world stops reading as “Yao” once a few high-frequency motifs disappear, that failure would reveal how much the model—and perhaps my own vision—depends on stereotype. These tests may fail. That is necessary. A concept becomes useful to me only when a work can disprove it.

Cinema without a set should still have consequences

The phrase “anything is possible” has become a common celebration of generative image making. I understand the excitement. I also think “anything is possible” is a poor artistic condition if nothing has consequence. Cinema needs consequence. A cut matters because something is lost. A camera position matters because another position is unavailable. A performance matters because a body committed to a moment. A set matters because it resists. A cultural reference matters because it belongs to history and relation. An image matters because the filmmaker decided not to replace it with another equally polished possibility. The challenge of post-camera cinema is therefore not to maximize freedom. It is to reconstruct consequence after constraint has been automated away. That is why I continue to need the language of the set, even when there is no physical set. I need some structure that tells the image it cannot become everything. The future I am interested in is not a cinema liberated from the world, but a cinema forced to invent new ways of answering to it.
