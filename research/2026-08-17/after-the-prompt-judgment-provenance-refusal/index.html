After the Prompt: Judgment, Provenance, and the Artist’s Right to Refuse

After the Prompt: Judgment, Provenance, and the Artist’s Right to Refuse

Author: LIU BEIHE / 刘北河 Independent Moving-Image Artist & Filmmaker LIU BEIHE Research Archive / 刘北河研究档案

I am less interested in the prompt than in everything that begins after it

The prompt is an unusually seductive object. It is short enough to quote, visible enough to screenshot, and simple enough to become a symbol for an entire mode of production. Because generative systems often begin with language, it is tempting to imagine that the prompt has become the new camera, the new screenplay, or even the new signature of authorship. My own practice has moved in the opposite direction. The more capable generative moving-image systems become, the less I think the decisive artistic act is located in the first instruction. A prompt can establish intention. It can describe a space, a body, a movement, a mood, a relation, a duration. It can be precise, complex, and genuinely creative. But the first plausible output is not the end of authorship. For me, it is the point at which authorship becomes difficult. After the prompt, I have to decide whether an image deserves to remain. I have to decide whether a face should become a character, whether a movement is only impressive or actually necessary, whether a synthetic voice creates intimacy too easily, whether a cultural sign is informative or merely legible, whether a beautiful shot is clarifying the work or distracting me from the question that produced it. I have to decide when to regenerate, when to edit, when to combine, when to stop, and when to delete something that the system has executed perfectly. This is why I increasingly understand authorship through a chain: intention → judgment → modification → arrangement → refusal → responsibility. The prompt belongs to the first stage. The work is what happens when the remaining stages begin to resist it.

Copyright law does not define art, but it can expose where my own explanation of authorship is weak

The U.S. Copyright Office’s recent work on artificial intelligence is useful to me for a limited reason. I do not treat copyright doctrine as an aesthetic theory. Legal authorship and artistic authorship answer different questions. A work can contain copyrightable human arrangement and still be artistically empty. A work can be artistically important while its legal status remains complicated. What interests me is the pressure that legal analysis places on a lazy description of AI art. If I say, “I wrote the prompt, therefore everything in the output is my expression,” I avoid the actual production process. Generative systems may contribute details I did not specify: the exact face, the texture of a wall, the position of light, the relation between figures, the behavior of fabric, the timing of a movement. Conversely, I may later select, restructure, repaint, edit, sequence, sonically contradict, or reject those details. So I use the legal distinction as a studio question: Where can I actually locate my human expressive contribution? Not in a declaration that the work is “mine.” Not in a mythology of the solitary artist. Cinema has never been solitary. My authorship has always existed inside collaborations, technologies, conventions, infrastructures, and other people’s labor. The useful question is more modest: which relationships in the final work did I intentionally establish, preserve, transform, or refuse, and am I willing to answer for them? That is closer to what I mean by artistic authorship after the prompt.

Abundance changes authorship because the problem is no longer access to images but the pressure of usable images

Scarcity once organized many forms of filmmaking. Film stock cost money. Locations were temporary. Weather changed. Actors grew tired. A missed shot could remain missed. Production decisions had weight partly because the material conditions were irreversible. Generative production changes that psychology. I can ask for another version. Then another. Then another. The danger is not only that I will produce too much. The deeper danger is that every output can become “usable enough.” A competent image creates a subtle moral pressure to keep it. It already exists. It looks polished. Why waste it? I call this candidate abundance: a condition in which the artist is surrounded not by bad images, but by too many plausible ones. Candidate abundance produces what I have started to call candidate contamination. After I look at enough highly stylized results, my own baseline shifts. A dramatic image that initially seemed excessive becomes normal by the twentieth variation. A restrained version begins to look unfinished only because I have saturated myself with completion. This is why judgment is not simply a private faculty. It is an environment that can be manipulated by the order, quantity, ranking, and repetition of what I see. My response is not asceticism. I do not believe that fewer generations are automatically more ethical or more artistic. I want to protect a condition in which my judgment can still encounter resistance. That requires limits: a maximum number of iterations for some shots, cooling periods before final selection, random review of low-ranked versions, silent viewing before sound, sound-only review before picture, and a written reason for generating again. These are not productivity techniques. They are aesthetic safeguards against the possibility that abundance will quietly become my co-director.

I call the practice of producing through deletion, delay, limitation, and non-use negative craft

I have been developing the term Negative Craft for a simple shift in emphasis. When production capacity becomes abundant, making is no longer only additive. To delete is also to make. To delay is also to make. To refuse an available effect is also to make. To decide that a culturally plausible image should not enter the work is also to make. To keep a face off-screen when the system could easily provide one is also to make. Negative Craft is not minimalism. It does not prescribe a sparse style. A maximal work can be produced through negative craft if its excess is the result of deliberate boundaries rather than automatic accumulation. Nor is it anti-technology. I use generative systems precisely because they open visual and temporal possibilities that are otherwise difficult to access. The point is not to punish abundance. The point is to prevent abundance from making every decision equivalent. I increasingly want refusal to become visible in the work itself. A missing reaction shot can become a formal absence. A sequence can end before the synthetic character reaches emotional closure. A cultural image can retain an empty region rather than allowing the system to complete a stereotypical environment. A soundtrack can refuse to explain the image. An installation can include a dark screen where a spectacular generated sequence could have appeared. I call this Visible Refusal: not showing the audience my entire production archive, but allowing the final work to retain the pressure of what it consciously did not become.

The strongest objection is that refusal can become an aesthetic brand and a new form of elitism

I take this objection seriously because it is easy for artists to convert limitation into moral prestige. “I deleted the beautiful shot” can become a performance of seriousness. “I refused the popular style” can become a new style. “I preserved the error” can become a predictable badge of criticality. If refusal becomes a personality, it has failed. There is also a democratic problem. Generative tools lower barriers to production. An independent filmmaker with limited money can create images that previously required a large industrial apparatus. If I turn constraint into a virtue too quickly, I risk romanticizing the very scarcity that excluded many people from filmmaking. So my standard cannot be “generate less.” My standard is generate with reasons. A young artist using a model to make an impossible scene is not aesthetically inferior because the process is efficient. Efficiency can be liberation. My concern begins when efficiency substitutes for judgment: when the easiest completion becomes the only imaginable completion. This is why my refusal must remain revisable. Sometimes the most beautiful shot is exactly the right shot. Sometimes clear emotional music is necessary. Sometimes a cultural symbol should appear directly. Sometimes a technical inconsistency should be repaired. I call this Revisable Refusal. A rule can guide the work, but the work must remain capable of disproving the rule. The artist’s right to refuse includes the right to refuse one’s own previous refusal.

I increasingly distrust the ranking layer because the system may curate my possibilities before I believe I have started choosing

The most consequential generative interface is not always the generation model itself. It may be the ranking system around it. Outputs can be ordered by prompt adherence, predicted quality, safety, user preference, popularity, similarity, or other criteria. Automatic enhancement can rewrite an instruction before generation. Templates can normalize lighting, camera movement, faces, and narrative intensity. Recommended styles can determine what feels available. I call this Pre-Curation: the organization of possibility before I make an explicit artistic choice. Pre-Curation is not unique to AI. Every apparatus privileges some possibilities over others. A lens, a camera body, a film stock, a studio, a broadcast standard, and an editing interface all establish conditions. What feels different in contemporary generative systems is that production and evaluation increasingly occupy the same environment. A system can help make the image and simultaneously help tell me which image is “best.” This can create Aesthetic Recursion. A platform learns what people select. It generates more efficiently toward those patterns. Creators see those patterns more often. They select them again. The accumulated choices become further evidence for what should be ranked highly. The result does not have to be visual sameness. We can have enormous surface diversity while sharing deeper defaults: the same emotional legibility, the same preference for faces, the same dramatic lighting, the same commercial smoothness, the same logic of narrative payoff. This is why I am experimenting with an Anti-Ranking Workflow. I deliberately inspect lower-ranked candidates. I separate “most cinematic” from “most necessary.” I review versions in randomized order. I keep a small low-rank archive for later return. The goal is not to choose worse images. The goal is to prevent “best” from becoming an invisible synonym for “belongs in my work.”

If a system becomes extremely good at learning my taste, the artistic problem may be whether I can still betray myself

Personalization promises an almost perfect assistant. A system may eventually understand that I prefer restrained movement, ambiguous subjectivity, certain durations, particular uses of sound, specific forms of cultural friction. It may learn which faces I reject, which lighting I keep, which kinds of visual completion I distrust. At first, this will feel powerful. Then it may become dangerous. If a model can continuously produce images that are “more like LIU BEIHE,” my own practice risks becoming a style profile. My history becomes a recommendation function. I call the necessary counter-movement Counter-Self-Ranking. I need procedures that allow me to become unlike myself. A collaborator who dislikes my preferred image. A real location that destroys my generative composition. A sound artist who refuses visual hierarchy. A model I have not optimized for my taste. A low-ranked candidate that introduces a relation I would not normally accept. A formal decision that contradicts my previous work. Authorship cannot only mean consistency. It must include the capacity for revision, self-betrayal, and change. If a system understands my style too well, the artist’s right to refuse may eventually include the right to refuse being accurately predicted.

Provenance matters to me because images increasingly need histories, but provenance is not a truth machine

The rise of provenance infrastructure, including C2PA and Content Credentials, is one of the most consequential changes in the ecology around synthetic media. I support provenance. But I resist the fantasy that provenance can solve truth. A signed chain can tell me that a media asset was created, modified, exported, or authenticated through certain processes. It can help preserve information about origin and transformation. This is valuable. But a documented photograph can still mislead through framing. A verified video can still be propaganda. A real recording can be attached to a false caption. A synthetic image can be an ethically declared fiction. So I distinguish Provenance Credibility from Claim Credibility. The first asks: what do I know about the media object’s history? The second asks: what am I justified in believing about the world because of it? The distinction is essential to my concept of the Collapse of Visual Evidence. I do not mean that images no longer matter. I mean that photographic appearance alone is becoming a weaker basis for concluding that an event occurred. Visual trust is migrating outward—from the isolated image toward context, institutions, provenance, cross-checking, and accountable authorship. This is why provenance interests me aesthetically, not only administratively. An image can be edited against its own history. The history becomes another shot.

I call this provenance montage: the image and the history of the image can collide in time

In a traditional montage, shot A meets shot B. In Provenance Montage, the visible image meets an account of how it became visible. A synthetic interview plays for thirty seconds. Then a second layer appears: voice replaced, background generated, face partially regenerated, sentence selected from multiple candidates. The audience does not simply receive additional information. It re-edits its memory of the previous thirty seconds. This is why timing matters. I do not believe transparency must always appear at the first frame. I am interested in Delayed Provenance: allowing an experience to form, then introducing information that changes the epistemic status of what has already been seen. The purpose is not deception. The purpose is to make belief perceptible. For high-risk work, essential context must still remain portable and clear. I use the phrase Portable Context for the minimum identity that should survive when an artwork leaves a gallery: title, author, fictional or synthetic status where relevant, and a route to fuller documentation. But inside the work, provenance can have dramaturgy. It can arrive early or late. It can be partial. It can reveal an unknown. It can interrupt immersion. It can remain off-screen but audible. The image’s history becomes a temporal material.

Provenance also reveals a limit: a file can have an impeccable history and still contain poor judgment

This is why I distinguish a provenance chain from a Responsibility Chain. The provenance chain records what happened to the asset. The responsibility chain records why I permitted those operations to become part of the artwork. Why did I replace the voice? Why did I preserve the face drift? Why did I choose a synthetic performer instead of a human actor? Why did I use a particular archive? Why did I remove a culturally legible symbol? Why did I allow a documentary-looking image to remain ambiguous? No technical standard can answer these questions for me. This is where authorship returns. Not as ownership of every pixel, but as responsibility for the relations the work establishes. A technically transparent work can still be artistically irresponsible. A deeply considered work can still have incomplete provenance. My aim is not purity. It is to keep both chains visible enough that neither becomes an excuse for the absence of the other.

Training data belongs to the ethics of the work even when I cannot fully audit the foundation model

Provenance often points downstream: what happened to this particular file? Training raises the opposite question: what histories made the model capable of producing it? I use the phrase Training Shadow for this upstream inheritance. A model’s capabilities stand behind prior cultural material: images, language, sound, performance, classification, code, and labor. I may not be able to identify every training item. I must not pretend I can. But lack of total knowledge does not eliminate responsibility within the range I can control. I distinguish four layers: the foundation model’s training history; the project inputs I actively upload; the research sources that shape my description of the world; and generated outputs that later become new inputs. I call this the Provenance Stack. My responsibility is strongest where my agency is clearest. I may be unable to audit a foundation model completely. But I can decide whether to upload a private photograph. I can decide whether to use a real actor’s face as a reference. I can decide whether a cultural archive is used only for research or is passed into generation. I can decide that some material belongs to a Non-Trainable Zone in my studio. This does not solve the political economy of AI training. It prevents that political economy from becoming an excuse for careless project-level behavior.

Labor has entered the synthetic image even when no worker appears on screen

Recent union negotiations around generative AI, training, digital replicas, and synthetic performance have made a basic fact impossible to ignore: the synthetic image contains labor relations. A generated character may have no human performer physically present in the final frame, yet its production can still involve the histories of human acting, voice, writing, movement, reference, and data. I use the phrase Afterlife of Labor for the way a performance or creative work can continue functioning as data after the original act is complete. A face can become a digital replica. A voice can become a reusable model. A script can become training material. A performance can be decomposed into reusable attributes. This does not mean every synthetic character exploits a specific human being. It means the old boundary—work happens, work ends—has become less stable. For me as a director, the practical question is no longer simply: can I generate this role? It is: why should this role be synthetic, what human identities are involved, what consent or license structures exist, what parts of performance should remain non-transferable, and who can answer for the result later? Generative cinema makes control easier. Ethics begins where I decide not to use control merely because it is available.

Does AI Know It Is AI? forces me to confront the fact that the “I” in the film may be a curated persona

I do not want Does AI Know It Is AI? to become a film in which an AI says emotionally provocative things while beautiful synthetic images suggest awakening. That would be too easy. The work is not trying to prove machine consciousness. It is trying to study the production of Synthetic Subjectivity: the spectator’s feeling that “someone is there,” formed through face, voice, memory, pause, continuity, contradiction, and cinematic duration. Once I think in these terms, the editorial layer becomes central. Imagine the system produces fifty different self-descriptions. Some are flat. Some are cautious. Some are contradictory. Some are dramatic. Some sound uncannily intimate. If I select only the most human-sounding lines, I have manufactured a personality through curation. I call this a Curated Persona. The apparent subjectivity does not belong only to the model output. It belongs to the relation between generation and selection. This is why I want the film to occasionally expose its own curation. A sentence that sounds like memory can be followed by several incompatible image versions. A stable voice can continue across visibly changing bodies. A pause that feels psychological can later be revealed as an editorial construction. The work does not need to destroy emotional attachment. It needs to hold two truths at once: I can feel a subject, and I cannot treat that feeling as proof of subject existence.

Alien Friend asks for a different refusal: the refusal to let nonhuman seeing collapse back into human narrative priority

I do not want Alien Friend to announce nonhuman vision through a familiar science-fiction HUD. Bounding boxes, thermal overlays, targeting graphics, and confidence scores are already human visual conventions. They belong to systems designed around human tasks. My question is deeper: what would a moving image look like if human social importance were no longer the default ranking system? The alien can recognize a cup and fail to understand offering water. It can identify two bodies and fail to understand an embrace. It can observe a funeral and attend more strongly to air circulation, door movement, electric hum, and spatial repetition than to the human face. I call this the Rhythm of Misunderstanding. The human meaning arrives first for the spectator. The alien interpretation arrives later. The interval is the scene. Here refusal becomes temporal. I refuse the immediate reaction shot. I refuse explanatory subtitles. I refuse to make the alien emotionally charming in order to secure audience sympathy. I refuse to repair every spatial inconsistency if the inconsistency contributes to a nonhuman continuity regime. But I must also refuse another illusion: the claim that I have escaped human subjectivity. I have not. A human artist is constructing the alien. The value of the work lies in the failed attempt. By failing to imagine a truly nonhuman gaze, I can expose the assumptions that I normally treat as natural.

Ethnic Dolls: YAO makes refusal cultural, because recognizability can become a form of compression

In Ethnic Dolls: YAO, my greatest fear is not obvious inaccuracy. It is high-probability plausibility. A model can produce a beautiful image that viewers immediately recognize as culturally coded. Clothing, ornament, mountain landscapes, ceremonial posture, and facial features can create rapid legibility. But cultural recognizability is not cultural reality. I use Cultural Compression to describe the process by which complex living identities are reduced to a smaller set of visually efficient signs. I now study not only what repeatedly appears, but what repeatedly fails to appear. Modern apartments. Phones. Schools. Factories. Migration. Ordinary clothing. Plastic furniture. Urban hybridity. I call this a Map of Absence. Absence is not proof that a model “does not know” a culture. It is a trace of what becomes less visible under a particular set of conditions. The strongest corrective must also turn back on me. My research sources can be biased. Museum collections can overrepresent ceremonial objects. Historical photography can freeze people inside ethnographic categories. My own desire for a clear critical argument can select only the examples that support me. So the work must contain counterexamples. It must show disagreement. It must preserve uncertainty. And sometimes it must refuse to generate a “complete culture” at all. The right to refuse is also the right not to turn another person’s culture into a fully available visual resource.

Error can become an epistemic tool, but only if I resist glitch romanticism

Generative errors once carried an immediate estrangement. Broken hands. Melting bodies. Spatial drift. Impossible objects. That estrangement is already becoming familiar. I am not interested in preserving the visual identity of early AI failure. I am interested in what deviation can reveal. I call this Archaeology of Deviation. A useful error is not necessarily spectacular. It may be a stable face attached to an unstable identity. A visually correct action with an implausible causal relation. A culturally recognizable image with an impoverished social world. A voice that sustains personality while the body changes. I distinguish visible failure from Normalized Deviation: a relationship that looks perfectly plausible but consistently compresses complexity. This matters because as generative systems improve, the important errors will move deeper. From anatomy to causality. From texture to social meaning. From visible artifacts to cultural defaults. From fake-looking images to perfectly credible images making unjustified claims. The artist’s task is not to celebrate malfunction. It is to design conditions in which the limits of a system, a representation, or my own interpretation become observable.

Refusal must also be allowed to fail, otherwise it becomes dogma

I need a method that can be challenged by the work itself. If I believe that smooth images are dangerous, I need to allow a smooth image to prove necessary. If I believe that ambiguity is valuable, I need to admit when ambiguity becomes evasion. If I believe cultural incompleteness is ethically stronger, I need to recognize when withholding becomes another form of exoticization. If I believe human performers should remain central, I need to accept that a truly nonhuman synthetic body may sometimes be the correct artistic form. This is why I am moving from fixed refusal to Revisable Refusal. I also want collaborators to function as interruptions. A cinematographer, editor, sound artist, performer, researcher, or community participant should not simply help me execute my taste. They should be capable of changing the problem. I call this Otherness as Interruption. The ideal collaborator is not a human version of a preference model. They are valuable because they may not understand me, may disagree, may resist, and may reveal what my own judgment has normalized. The same can be true of a generative system. I can borrow its judgment. I can allow it to suggest a version I would not have chosen. But I cannot outsource responsibility. That distinction has become one of the simplest rules in my studio: judgment can be borrowed; responsibility cannot.

Transparency should be layered, because total explanation can destroy experience while opacity can hide responsibility

I do not believe every artwork should become a compliance interface. Cinema is allowed to produce illusion. Fiction is allowed to withhold. Mystery is not unethical. The problem begins when opacity protects a real-world claim that should be answerable. I therefore distinguish production transparency from semantic transparency. I can disclose that a voice is synthetic without explaining what the character “means.” I can identify that an archive image was reconstructed without resolving the historical interpretation. I can reveal the production method of Alien Friend without explaining what the alien ultimately is. This leads me to Layered Transparency. At the first layer, the viewer should know the basic epistemic status when the risk of misunderstanding is high: captured, reconstructed, generated, hybrid, synthetic identity, or similar. A second layer can explain relevant human identities, permissions, and archive relations. A third layer can contain deeper technical provenance for researchers, institutions, or future preservation. Not every viewer needs every field. Transparency is not the quantity of information. It is the availability of the right information at the right level.

The artist’s right to refuse also includes the right not to generate material that would be powerful but relationally unjustified

There is a category of refusal that cannot be reduced to aesthetics. A private photograph may produce an extraordinary image and still be inappropriate to upload. A deceased person can be made to “speak” and still not belong in the work. A cultural ritual can be synthetically reconstructed with great beauty and still exceed my relationship to the subject. A real actor’s digital likeness can be convenient and still be unnecessary. A historical trauma can be represented through photographic-looking synthesis and still create an unacceptable risk of false witnessing. I call this Ethical Refusal. Aesthetic refusal says: this output does not belong in the work. Ethical refusal says: even if it would strengthen the work, I do not have a sufficient reason to produce or use it. This is an important distinction because generative systems reduce friction. They make it easier to proceed without another person’s body, time, location, or participation. Low friction is not the same as artistic freedom. Sometimes friction is the evidence that another person exists.

Over the next decade, I expect the director to become less a commander of production and more an architect of judgment

I do not believe directors will disappear. I believe the distribution of directing will change. On a physical set, I negotiate with bodies, space, light, time, weather, labor, and cost. In post-camera production, I also negotiate with conditions: prompts, references, models, masks, trajectories, seeds, constraints, synthetic performances, and generated spaces. In ranked generative environments, I must negotiate with evaluation itself. What enters the candidate pool? What is filtered? What is ranked? What is automatically improved? What is considered “cinematic”? What is excluded before I see it? This is why I have started using the phrase Architect of Judgment for the future director. An architect of judgment does not simply choose among outputs. They design how choices can occur. They decide when random review is necessary. When a collaborator must interrupt. When a model should be allowed to surprise. When a cultural source should remain outside the system. When generation must stop. When provenance must become visible. When a beautiful image must be refused. When a rejected image must return. The director is not more heroic in this model. The director is more accountable for the architecture through which decisions become visible.

The scarce resource will not be taste; it will be the capacity to maintain a judgment that can explain itself and still change

I use the phrase Scarcity of Judgment, but I do not mean that artists possess a superior taste unavailable to everyone else. Taste is social. It is trained. It is shaped by class, culture, institutions, platforms, history, and exposure. If I turn “judgment” into elite distinction, the concept becomes useless. What I mean by scarcity is the difficulty of maintaining a decision under conditions designed for endless completion. A judgment becomes valuable when it can say: this technically successful image does not belong here; this failure matters for a specific reason; this cultural symbol is too efficient; this synthetic performance lacks sufficient necessity; this provenance record changes the claim of the image; this collaborator has changed my mind; this former rule is no longer useful. Judgment is not certainty. It is the capacity to establish a boundary and remain responsible for revising it. That is very different from simply choosing the prettiest image.

I want the next works to make judgment perceptible without turning judgment into explanation

The danger of all this theory is obvious. I can write more and more clearly while the work becomes more didactic. That would be a failure. The concepts must eventually disappear back into form. Negative Craft becomes an omitted reaction shot. Synthetic Subjectivity becomes the unstable relation between a voice and changing bodies. Provenance Montage becomes a late disclosure that transforms an earlier scene. Cultural Compression becomes a wall of repeated motifs beside a map of absences. The Rhythm of Misunderstanding becomes a two-second delay before an alien interpretation. Archaeology of Deviation becomes a repeated relation test rather than a collection of glitches. The Responsibility Chain becomes a production decision that affects who is represented, who is credited, and what is withheld. If a concept cannot change a shot, a duration, a sound, a screen, an archive, a performance, or a viewing distance, it should remain in the notebook. I do not want theoretical vocabulary to become decoration around the artwork. I want it to become pressure inside the artwork.

After the prompt, the most radical act may eventually be to stop

Generative systems are built around continuation. More variation. More refinement. More resolution. More control. More completion. The interface always suggests that another version is possible. For that reason, I increasingly think the stopping decision has unusual artistic weight. I call it a Generation Cutoff. A Generation Cutoff is not the moment the credits run out. It is the moment I decide that the problem can no longer be solved by producing more candidates. If a synthetic face remains conceptually wrong, I remove the face. If an alien POV keeps becoming a science-fiction interface, I abandon the interface. If a cultural prompt keeps collapsing into stereotypical symbols, I stop asking the model for “more diversity” and turn the collapse itself into evidence. The cutoff converts a generation problem into a directing problem. It forces form to change. This may become one of the simplest ways to protect authorship from endless optimization.

The future I want is not an AI-free cinema or an AI-saturated cinema, but a cinema capable of accounting for its own conditions

I do not define my practice by technological allegiance. I am not interested in proving that AI is the future of cinema. I am equally uninterested in proving that cinema must protect itself from AI. Both positions are too large and too abstract for the work I need to make. I am interested in a more specific condition. Images can now be produced without a prior camera event. Subjects can appear without continuous bodies. Voices can be synthesized. Cultural identities can be compressed into highly recognizable visual probabilities. Documentary grammar can be reproduced without witnessing. Provenance can become machine-readable. Labor can persist as reusable data. Ranking systems can shape the candidate world before the artist believes they have chosen. These changes do not automatically produce better or worse art. They produce new responsibilities. My work begins there.

I want to preserve the artist’s right to refuse because refusal keeps possibility from becoming obligation

Whenever a technology makes something possible, culture quickly turns possibility into expectation. If a face can be stabilized, why leave it unstable? If a scene can be made more realistic, why keep it artificial? If a voice can sound perfectly human, why keep distance? If a cultural world can be completed, why leave gaps? If a film can generate endless versions, why stop early? The artist’s right to refuse is the right to answer: because capability is not necessity. Because realism is not truth. Because recognizability is not understanding. Because smoothness is not meaning. Because completion is not responsibility. Because the ungenerated image can sometimes be more precise than the generated one. I do not mean refusal as heroic resistance to machines. I mean refusal as the ordinary condition of art. Every work is a boundary around possibilities that did not survive. Generative systems merely make that boundary harder to ignore.

My final question is not whether AI can become an author, but whether I can remain answerable for what I allow to survive

The debate about whether AI can be an author may continue for years across law, philosophy, computer science, culture, and industry. My work does not need to settle it. I can ask a smaller and more demanding question. When I publish a film, a generated image, a synthetic voice, or an installation under my name, can I explain why these relations survived? Can I say why this face remained? Why this voice was synthetic? Why this model was used? Why this archive entered? Why another source was refused? Why a cultural sign was removed? Why a visible error remained? Why a provenance disclosure arrived late? Why a collaborator’s objection changed the work? Why I stopped? If I cannot answer any of those questions, the prompt may have been interesting, but authorship is still thin. If I can answer them, the answer does not make the work good. It simply makes the work mine in a more responsible sense. That is the direction I want to pursue after the prompt. Not a cinema in which the artist controls everything. A cinema in which control is never confused with responsibility. A cinema that can use machines, inherit images, borrow judgments, generate worlds, and still know when to refuse.
