Machine Seeing, Authorship, and the Scarcity of Judgment

Machine Seeing, Authorship, and the Scarcity of Judgment

> Author：LIU BEIHE / 刘北河
Independent Moving-Image Artist & Filmmaker
LIU BEIHE Research Archive / 刘北河研究档案

I am less worried that machines will make images than that images will stop being scarce

For most of the history of moving images, production imposed friction. A photograph required a camera and a scene. A film required time, bodies, light, locations, equipment, money, and coordination. Even digital production, despite lowering many costs, still forced filmmakers to confront the physical and organizational limits of making images. Generative systems alter this relation. The number of possible images I can produce is beginning to exceed the number of images I could ever meaningfully consider. This changes the artistic problem. When images are scarce, production skill has enormous value. When images become abundant, another capacity becomes more important: the ability to decide what should not exist in the work. I call this condition the Scarcity of Judgment. The phrase does not mean that judgment has disappeared. It means that judgment becomes more valuable precisely because visual possibility becomes cheap. A model can give me ten versions, then a hundred, then a thousand. It can propose different faces, lighting conditions, camera movements, environments, and emotional registers. The apparent creative freedom is enormous. But freedom is not the same as art. If I cannot explain why one possibility belongs to the work and the others do not, abundance has not expanded my authorship. It has only expanded my menu.

Machine Seeing changes authorship because the system already arrives with visual preferences

I do not think of generative AI as an empty tool waiting for my intention. A model has been trained on a vast history of images and descriptions. It has learned statistical relationships among faces, bodies, composition, genre, color, cinematography, design, and language. When I ask for an image, I am not creating inside an empty field. I enter a probability space that has already been shaped by cultural repetition. This is why my idea of Machine Seeing matters to authorship. Machine Seeing, as I use the term, is not the claim that an algorithm possesses a human-like visual consciousness. It describes a system in which images are automatically recognized, classified, valued, predicted, and generated according to learned structures. CLIP is a useful historical example. OpenAI trained the model on large-scale image–text relations so that visual concepts could be associated with natural language categories and used for zero-shot classification. [1] The model did not need human visual experience to reorganize a visual field into machine-readable relations. Generative models extend this from classification toward production. The important artistic consequence is that the machine does not merely execute my vision. It continuously suggests visual normality. Certain faces arrive more easily. Certain cinematic lighting schemes feel immediately “successful.” Certain ways of representing beauty, futurity, tradition, luxury, tragedy, ethnicity, or technological sophistication recur with remarkable stability. I therefore cannot treat the first convincing output as neutral. The first convincing output may simply be the place where the model’s learned culture is most confident.

A beautiful image can be the point where my authorship is weakest

This is one of the most uncomfortable lessons I have learned from generative work. The image that looks most finished is often the image I trust least. A spectacular result can arrive before I have developed a real artistic position. It can contain cinematic light, expensive-looking texture, emotional facial expression, and an apparently deliberate composition. Because it resembles the visual language of professional film and advertising, it produces an immediate feeling of accomplishment. But visual completion can precede conceptual completion. The image is “good” before I know why it needs to exist. This reverses a familiar production sequence. Traditionally, an artist might struggle for a long time to make an image approximate an intention. Generative systems can produce images that exceed the visible specificity of the intention almost immediately. The danger is that I then allow the image to retroactively define the work. I begin to invent reasons for keeping it because it is impressive. For me, this is one of the central authorship problems of generative art. I need a method capable of saying no to technically successful images.

I call that method Negative Craft

I use the term Negative Craft for a practice built around refusal, deletion, constraint, and exclusion. Traditional craft is often recognized through what an artist can make. Negative Craft is recognized through what an artist consistently refuses to allow into the work. I refuse an image because its beauty is too generic. I refuse a camera movement because it announces technical virtuosity without changing the idea. I refuse a synthetic face because it turns cultural identity into decoration. I refuse a “glitch” because it has become a ready-made symbol for artificial intelligence. I refuse a dramatic transformation because it makes the model the protagonist instead of the work. These refusals are not acts of technological purism. They are a way of rebuilding friction. When the tool says yes too easily, the artist has to become capable of saying no.

Selection alone is not authorship

At first I thought of this shift as Authorship by Selection. That term is still useful, but I no longer think it is sufficient. Everyone who generates ten images and picks one has selected. Selection by itself does not create a meaningful authorial position. The important question is whether selections form a coherent structure of judgment over time. Why do I repeatedly reject one kind of visual solution? Why does a decision in one work connect to a decision in another? What does my editing reveal about the questions I am willing to keep unresolved? An authorial method emerges when individual choices become a durable way of organizing the world. This is why I now think:

Authorship is not the number of decisions I make. It is the continuity that makes those decisions belong to the same world.

Film direction has always been closer to judgment than to solitary production

Generative systems make this issue visible, but it is not entirely new. A film director has never personally manufactured every component of a film. Actors perform. Cinematographers expose images. Production designers build spaces. Editors make cuts. Sound designers produce acoustic worlds. Composers shape time through music. The romantic idea that authorship requires physically making every element has never described cinema very well. Directing is already an art of distributed production and concentrated judgment. The director establishes relations among contributions. The problem with auteur mythology is not that authorship does not exist. The problem is that authorship can be used to erase the labor of collaborators. Generative AI introduces a new version of this tension. A single creator may appear to make a film alone, while the underlying system depends on immense fields of training data, engineering, infrastructure, and prior cultural labor. This means I need two ideas at once: I can claim responsibility for the work without pretending I produced its entire cultural and technical substrate alone.

Responsibility is the part of authorship I cannot delegate to the model

This is why I am cautious when people call AI a “co-author.” The term can be provocative and useful in an artwork, but it can also blur an important distinction. A model can materially contribute output. It can surprise me. It can propose combinations I did not explicitly imagine. But it cannot assume responsibility for the cultural and ethical consequences of the finished work in the same way I can. MIT Press currently does not allow AI tools to be listed as authors, explicitly noting that such tools cannot take legal and ethical responsibility for scholarly work; human authors remain responsible for AI-assisted content and are expected to disclose relevant use. [2] Copyright law is different from publishing ethics, and neither defines art. But the distinction is useful. Contribution is not identical to authorship. For my own practice, authorship becomes strongest where responsibility becomes unavoidable. If I publish a synthetic cultural stereotype, I cannot say, “the model did it.” If I use an invented image in a documentary context, I cannot blame the tool for the viewer’s confusion. If I create a synthetic subject that invites emotional attachment, I am responsible for the conditions under which that attachment is produced. The system generates possibilities. I authorize one of those possibilities to enter the world as my work. That authorization matters.

Legal debates are revealing because they force expressive control to become concrete

The U.S. Copyright Office’s 2025 report on AI-generated outputs is useful to me not because copyright law can tell artists what authorship means, but because it separates several actions that are often collapsed in public discussion. The Office concluded that the use of AI does not automatically prevent copyright protection, but copyrightable authorship still depends on sufficient human expressive contribution. Merely providing prompts is generally not enough by itself; human-authored material, creative arrangement, and modification can matter. [3] The legal question is narrower than the artistic question, but both point toward control and responsibility rather than mere causation. Who caused an image to appear is not necessarily the same as who authored the work in a meaningful sense. This is exactly the distinction I need in my studio. I am not interested in proving that a prompt is legally sophisticated. I am interested in locating the moments where the work becomes mine in a deeper sense.

I have started keeping an Authorship Ledger

To make this less abstract, I use the idea of an Authorship Ledger. It is not a public manifesto and not a legal defense document. It is a studio method for tracking decisions. For an important work I want to preserve questions such as: What was the original problem? What did the first outputs get wrong? Which recurring visual tendencies came from the model? What did I reject? Where did I return to live action? Where did I deliberately retain a generative failure? What changed in the sound design? Which references altered my thinking? Where did the project contradict its original premise? The point is not administrative completeness. The ledger protects me from confusing output history with artistic history. A folder may contain hundreds of files. Only some decisions actually changed the work. Those changes are where authorship becomes visible to me.

The Writers Guild negotiations reveal that authorship is also a labor relation

There is another reason I resist treating authorship as a purely philosophical question. Authorship determines credit, compensation, bargaining power, and control. The Writers Guild of America’s 2026 MBA preserved its earlier AI protections and added a requirement that companies notify the Guild in writing when writers’ work is licensed to train commercial generative AI systems for output production; the Guild can request discussions concerning such licensing, including remuneration. [4] This matters because training changes the life cycle of creative work. A script is no longer valuable only as a script. It can become part of a machine’s future productive capacity. The labor question is therefore not only, “Will AI write the next script?” It is also, “Whose previous writing becomes the infrastructure through which future writing is produced?” This is a profound shift in cultural labor. It means the politics of authorship extend backward into training and forward into model outputs.

Machine Seeing is inseparable from the politics of what becomes visible

Authorship in generative imagery cannot be separated from Machine Seeing because the model’s possibility space is uneven. Some cultures, bodies, objects, and styles are highly visible in training distributions. Others are sparse, mislabeled, or filtered through external representation. UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence explicitly identifies cultural and linguistic diversity as areas in which AI systems can both enable and threaten pluralism. [5] Its later work on AI and culture emphasizes cultural sovereignty, algorithmic homogenization, platform concentration, and human creative agency. [6] This changes the meaning of artistic selection. If I generate a cultural identity and choose from the results, my choice is occurring inside a distribution already shaped by historical visibility. I am not selecting from the entire world. I am selecting from what the system has learned to make available. This is why my authorship has to include an awareness of the limits of the menu.

Ethnic Dolls: YAO taught me that an artist can become an amplifier of the model’s stereotype

In Ethnic Dolls: YAO, I initially encountered the seductive speed with which a model can create culturally recognizable figures. Recognition feels like success. But the more immediately a character signals a cultural identity through costume, ornament, color, and stylized facial features, the more I have to ask whether the image is culturally rich or merely statistically legible. This led me to the idea of Cultural Compression: complex cultural life can be reduced into a small set of highly visible symbols because those symbols are easiest for image systems and viewers to recognize. My role as author is not automatically critical simply because I notice the problem. If I choose the most beautiful compressed image, I may simply amplify it. Judgment therefore has to include the willingness to make the image less immediately consumable. Sometimes the more responsible authorial decision is to reduce recognizability.

Alien Friend taught me that authorship can reside in attention rather than objects

In Alien Friend, I do not want authorship to be demonstrated by the originality of a creature design. The deeper authorial decision is to reorganize what the film considers important. I call this Nonhuman Gaze. Rather than adding a visual filter that announces an alien point of view, I want to change the distribution of attention: human faces become less central; environmental repetition, material change, spatial edges, and non-narrative durations gain importance. A model can generate countless alien bodies. But the structure that determines what deserves screen time is not automatically generated. This is where authorship moves from object production toward temporal judgment.

Does AI Know It Is AI? taught me that authorship is also the design of uncertainty

In Does AI Know It Is AI?, the temptation would be to make a synthetic character say profound things about machine consciousness. I reject that. The work is stronger when it does not claim to prove AI consciousness at all. Instead, I use the term Synthetic Subjectivity to describe how images, voices, memory cues, and behavioral continuity can make a viewer feel that an interior subject exists. The authorial act lies partly in what I refuse to resolve. I do not tell the viewer whether the machine “really feels.” I design the conditions in which the viewer catches themselves assigning mental states to something they know is constructed. This has taught me that authorship is not always about making a clear statement. It can also be the disciplined construction of an unresolved question.

The abundance of outputs makes editing more philosophical, not less

Editing has always been an art of exclusion. A film is defined by the footage left out as much as by the footage included. Generative production radicalizes this principle because the excluded material is potentially infinite. There is no final roll of film that tells me production has ended. I can always regenerate. Change the prompt. Change the seed. Change the image. Try another model. Continue indefinitely. This means “cutting” begins before the timeline. The director edits the space of possibility itself. I think this will become one of the defining disciplines of generative filmmaking. The ability to stop will become an aesthetic skill.

I do not want “AI aesthetics” to become a genre

Every new technology produces recognizable visual habits. Early HDR photography, drone footage, digital lens flares, glitch art, VR environments — technical capacities quickly become stylistic conventions. AI imagery is already developing its own clichés: impossible morphs, liquid bodies, extreme texture, seamless transformation, synthetic perfection, deliberately broken anatomy, and visual references to technological interfaces. There is nothing inherently wrong with these languages. But I do not want my work to be identifiable primarily because it “looks AI-generated.” If a technology becomes a style, the style will age as quickly as the interface. I want the conceptual problem to survive model obsolescence. Machine Seeing should still matter when today’s models are forgotten. Post-Camera Cinema should still matter when generative continuity becomes technically ordinary. The Scarcity of Judgment should become more relevant, not less, as production gets easier.

Counter-Training Aesthetics: I want to work where the model becomes uncertain

My thinking about cultural homogenization has led me to another working term: Counter-Training Aesthetics. I do not mean technically retraining a model against itself. I mean deliberately making work in zones where the model’s learned confidence becomes unstable. Local linguistic nuance. Ambiguous cultural identity. Unfashionable visual conditions. Personal memory with no public image archive. Bodies that resist commercial idealization. Narrative events that do not resolve into familiar dramatic form. If the model immediately knows how to make an image “beautiful,” I become suspicious. I want to know what happens where the machine cannot easily complete my intention with its existing cultural averages. This is where artistic friction returns.

The future director may be defined by constraints rather than commands

Prompt culture encourages the image of a director as someone who gives increasingly precise instructions to a machine. I suspect the mature practice will look different. The strongest directors may not be those who command the model most effectively, but those who construct the most meaningful constraints around it. Do not show the face. Do not resolve the transformation. Do not make the image more cinematic. Use live-action sound against a generated body. Retain a continuity failure. Stop after three versions. Never generate a sacred object without documented contextual research. These constraints create authorship because they resist the system’s default invitation to continue optimizing. In this sense, the future director may resemble a curator, editor, system designer, and ethicist at once.

There is a danger in turning “human judgment” into another comforting slogan

I also need to criticize my own argument. It is easy to say: machines generate, humans judge. That distinction can become sentimental. Humans make bad judgments. Humans reproduce stereotypes. Humans chase trends. Human taste is trained by markets and platforms. There is nothing automatically superior about a human selection simply because it is human. The value of judgment therefore depends on its quality. A meaningful judgment should be historically informed, ethically aware, aesthetically specific, and capable of self-criticism. This is why research matters to my practice. If I refuse a generated cultural image because it “feels wrong,” that intuition may be useful, but it is not enough. I need to understand the history of representation, the cultural context, the model’s likely sources of visual shorthand, and my own position as an external viewer. Judgment has to be trained. The fact that humans retain responsibility does not guarantee that humans deserve authority. Authority has to be earned through method.

Scarcity of Judgment is therefore also a demand for a more rigorous artist

The phrase could be misunderstood as self-congratulation: images are cheap, therefore artists are more important. I mean almost the opposite. If visual production becomes easy, the excuse of technical difficulty disappears. The artist has fewer places to hide. A weak idea can no longer be defended by how hard the image was to make. A generic aesthetic can no longer be impressive merely because it required expensive production. A director who can produce any image has to answer a more severe question: Why this image? That makes the standard higher. It demands stronger research, clearer ethics, more precise formal decisions, and a longer continuity of thought across works.

I want my archive to make that continuity visible

This is one reason I am building the LIU BEIHE Research Archive alongside the works themselves. I do not want essays to function as explanatory packaging around finished art. I want writing to record how concepts evolve, contradict themselves, and produce new works. Machine Seeing leads toward Nonhuman Gaze. Nonhuman Gaze changes how I return to Alien Friend. Synthetic Subjectivity changes how I understand generated performers. Collapse of Visual Evidence leads toward Evidence of Events That Never Happened. Scarcity of Judgment and Negative Craft lead toward Rejected Cinema. The archive is therefore not a press kit. It is part of the practice. If authorship is continuity among judgments, the archive makes that continuity inspectable.

Over the next decade, I think the question will shift from “Can AI create?” to “Who can still establish value?”

The question “Can AI make art?” already feels less useful to me than it once did. Systems can generate cultural artifacts that people find beautiful, moving, strange, useful, or commercially successful. The philosophical debate over whether that counts as genuine machine creativity will continue, but artistic practice cannot wait for a final definition. The more urgent question is what happens when cultural production vastly exceeds cultural attention. If billions of images, songs, scenes, stories, and synthetic identities can be generated continuously, then production is no longer the primary bottleneck. Attention is limited. Interpretation is limited. Trust is limited. Institutional memory is limited. Human life is limited. Judgment determines what enters those limited spaces. This is why I think the Scarcity of Judgment will become a defining cultural condition. Not because humans will always judge better than machines. But because value can never be reduced entirely to the existence of more options. At some point someone, or some institution, has to say: This matters. This does not. This belongs together. This must remain unresolved. This should not be made. This deserves time. For my own practice, authorship begins there. Not at the moment the image appears, but at the moment I become responsible for why it remains.
