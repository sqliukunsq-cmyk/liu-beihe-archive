The Image That Never Happened: Cinema, Provenance, and the Ethics of Synthetic Witnessing

The Image That Never Happened: Cinema, Provenance, and the Ethics of Synthetic Witnessing

作者 / Author：刘北河 LIU BEIHE 独立影像艺术家、导演 / Independent Moving-Image Artist & Filmmaker LIU BEIHE Research Archive / 刘北河研究档案

The image that never happened is not automatically a lie

I want to begin with a distinction that has become fundamental to my work. An event can fail to have happened and an image can still be honest. Fiction has always depended on this possibility. An actor dies on screen without dying. A city is destroyed in a film while remaining intact outside the frame. A character remembers a childhood that belongs to no real person. Cinema has never required factual occurrence in order to produce emotional or philosophical truth. What generative moving image changes is not the legitimacy of fiction. It changes the ease with which a nonexistent event can inherit the sensory grammar of factual capture. A scene that was never photographed can look like a phone recording. A person who never lived can appear in an interview. A building that never stood can appear in surveillance footage. A war that never occurred can acquire compression artifacts, timestamps, field-recording noise, shaky focus, accidental framing, and the visual fatigue associated with evidence. I call such a work the image that never happened: an image whose narrative event is visible even though no corresponding capture event took place. The ethical problem begins only when the work asks for a kind of belief it has not earned. This is why I reject the easy equation: Generated = false. Captured = true. The more useful question is: what is this image claiming, and what kind of evidence supports that claim?

Cinema has always separated narrative events from real events, but synthetic media can now separate them from photographic events as well

In conventional fiction, the narrative event and the real event are already different. A performer pretends to be injured. The fictional character is injured. The actor is not. But a photographic event still occurred: a camera, a performer, a set, light, time, and a recording process occupied a real relation. Generative moving images allow a further separation. No performer is injured. No performer necessarily performs the injury. No camera necessarily records a body falling. A generation event produces the photographic appearance of a fall. The narrative world still contains the fall. I therefore distinguish three events: Capture Event — a recording system receives information from a physical situation in time. Generation Event — a computational process produces an audiovisual sequence under conditions. Narrative Event — something is understood to have happened within the world of the work. This distinction is simple, but I find it powerful because it prevents the word “real” from doing too many jobs at once. A fictional film can have a real capture event and an unreal narrative event. A fully generated film can have a real generation event and an unreal narrative event. A documentary can have a real capture event while making a misleading narrative claim. A reconstruction can have no original capture event for the reconstructed moment while honestly representing evidence about the past. The ethical question is therefore not whether these layers are different. Cinema has always lived among differences. The question is whether the work allows those differences to be mistaken for one another in consequential ways.

I use the phrase Collapse of Visual Evidence carefully because I do not believe evidence itself has collapsed

When I began developing the idea of Collapse of Visual Evidence, it was tempting to formulate it dramatically: if any image can be generated, visual evidence is dead. I no longer accept that formulation. Evidence is not a magical property stored inside pixels. A photograph becomes evidence through relations. Who made it? Where was it made? When? What does it show? What does it fail to show? What other records support it? Who preserves it? Which institution is willing to answer for it? Visual evidence has always required an ecology. What is collapsing is a shortcut: photographic appearance can no longer automatically stand in for a prior photographic event. A low-resolution, poorly framed, time-stamped image once carried a strong cultural suggestion that somebody or some device had been present. That suggestion is becoming technically detachable from presence. This is a profound change, but it is not the end of fact. It means evidence must become more relational.

Provenance is necessary precisely because the image surface has become less informative about production history

I support C2PA and Content Credentials for a practical reason. Digital media lose history easily. Files are copied, resized, cropped, transcoded, screenshotted, downloaded, and reposted. Production histories disappear while surfaces remain. Provenance standards attempt to preserve claims about creation, editing, ingredients, signatures, and transformations in a form that can travel with an asset. For a post-camera cinema, this is extremely important. If an image no longer tells me from appearance whether a camera was involved, the production history has to be recoverable somewhere else. But I do not treat provenance as a truth machine. A perfectly documented generated image can still make a false historical claim. A perfectly documented photograph can still be misleadingly captioned. A historical image without modern credentials can still be supported by strong archival evidence. Provenance gives me media history, not automatic event truth. That distinction protects me from two errors at once: rejecting provenance because it cannot solve everything, and worshipping provenance because it solves something important.

My central ethical concept is not “do not fabricate”; it is “do not borrow unearned authority”

Art fabricates. Cinema fabricates. The problem is not fabrication itself. The problem is unearned authority. If I create a nonexistent archive photograph and present it inside an explicitly fictional installation, I am using fabrication as artistic form. If I release the same image on a public platform with a false historical caption, I am asking the image to possess evidentiary authority it has not earned. The pixels can be identical. The ethics are different because the claim is different. This leads me to a working principle: The more a synthetic image borrows from evidentiary grammar, the more carefully its claim environment must be designed. Handheld instability is not neutral. A timestamp is not neutral. A surveillance angle is not neutral. Compression artifacts are not neutral. A documentary interview frame is not neutral. They carry histories of how society has learned to trust images. Generative cinema can use them, but it should know what it is borrowing.

Evidence of Events That Never Happened is the work in which I want to test this directly

I am developing a project under the working title Evidence of Events That Never Happened. Every central event in the project would be fictional. The audience would be informed before entering that the events did not occur. This is not a limitation. It is the beginning of the experiment. If viewers already know the events are fictional, why do certain images still feel more evidentiary than others? Why does a poor phone recording feel more like a witness than a perfect cinematic shot? Why does a timestamp create institutional confidence? Why does archival grain make an invented event feel historically distant rather than newly fabricated? Why does a hesitant voice feel more trustworthy than a fluent explanation? I want the work to test evidentiary sensation after factual belief has already been suspended. That allows me to study trust without deceiving the public about a real event.

I want the installation to contain several incompatible evidence systems

One screen could present “raw” footage. A second could present a reconstruction. A third could present an institutional diagram explaining the same fictional event. A fourth could present a synthetic witness interview. A fifth could present provenance records. None would be designated as the master truth. Instead, the spectator would notice that different forms produce different kinds of confidence. The shaky phone image feels contingent. The diagram feels analytical. The interview feels human. The provenance interface feels technical. The cinematic reconstruction feels coherent. Each form has a different authority. I call this structure Evidence Plurality. The installation does not ask, “Which one is fake?” All belong to the same fiction. It asks, “Why do you trust these forms differently?”

The synthetic witness is dangerous when fluency replaces embodied consequence

A human witness is not merely a sensor. The witness can be afraid. Can misremember. Can be injured. Can refuse to speak. Can suffer because they were present. A synthetic witness can produce a perfectly coherent account without paying the embodied cost of witnessing. I call this Costless Testimony. The phrase does not mean synthetic testimony is useless. A system can summarize evidence, visualize a reconstruction, or articulate a hypothetical perspective. The danger begins when coherence itself is treated as proof of lived experience. A real person may be fragmented precisely because the event was traumatic. A generated witness can be calm, complete, chronological, and persuasive because its testimony is constructed for readability. If public culture rewards fluency, the synthetic witness can become more credible in appearance than the vulnerable human witness. This is not only a technological issue. It is a cinematic one. Film has always known how to make a speaker credible through lighting, framing, sound, and duration. Generative systems make the speaker itself synthesizable.

Does AI Know It Is AI? lets me study testimony turned inward

In Does AI Know It Is AI?, the witness is not describing an external event. It appears to describe itself. “I remember.” “I am uncertain.” “I was afraid.” “I do not know why I answered that way.” These statements have the grammar of introspection. But the presence of introspective language does not prove the presence of subjective experience. This is why I use Synthetic Subjectivity as a distinct concept. Synthetic Subjectivity describes the spectator’s experience that “someone” seems to be present behind the audiovisual surface. It does not settle machine consciousness. The relationship to synthetic witnessing is direct. A witness says, “I saw.” A synthetic subject says, “I felt.” Both statements can become extremely persuasive through cinematic form. Both require the artist to distinguish between the appearance of testimony and the epistemic status of the claim.

I want to make a self-testimony scene in which every formal cue says “inner life” while the archive says “constructed”

The character sits in a fixed medium close-up. It speaks quietly. The sound contains small breaths. The lighting is soft but not theatrical. The character pauses before answering. The camera does not cut away. Everything in film language asks the spectator to read an interior state. Behind the viewing room, another screen shows the production history: voice components, face versions, memory design, edit points, generated pauses. The two screens do not cancel each other. The first screen produces subjectivity. The second produces provenance. The spectator has to hold both. I call this Double Knowledge: knowing that a subject effect is constructed while continuing to experience it emotionally. Cinema has always worked this way with fiction. Generative characters make the construction more materially explicit.

Alien Friend gives me the opposite problem: perfect recording without human meaning

In Alien Friend, I want to imagine a nonhuman observer that records human events with extraordinary technical fidelity and still misunderstands them. The system knows the time. The location. The acoustic spectrum. The temperature. The bodies present. Nothing in the record has been falsified. Yet a funeral is classified as collective stillness. An embrace is interpreted as temporary bodily constraint. A whispered apology is less salient than a fluctuation in wall temperature. The record is accurate. The meaning is poor. This gives me another distinction: Record Accuracy is not Interpretive Adequacy. Provenance strengthens the first. It cannot automatically provide the second. I think this is one of the most important lessons for a future saturated with machine-readable media history. We can know exactly where an image came from and still misunderstand what happened.

Ethnic Dolls: YAO demonstrates how recognizability can impersonate evidence

Cultural images create another form of evidentiary seduction. A generated figure looks “right.” The clothing is recognizable. The ornaments are recognizable. The background is recognizable. An outside viewer concludes that the image is culturally accurate. But recognizability can reflect stereotype, public-image frequency, tourism, museum display, or training-data concentration rather than living cultural complexity. This is why I use the concept Cultural Compression. A complex culture becomes a limited visual vocabulary optimized for rapid classification. The resulting image can look more “authentic” to an outsider because it contains more of the signs the outsider expects. Here again, appearance is functioning as evidence. The work must interrupt that shortcut. In Ethnic Dolls: YAO, I want to compare high-frequency generated forms with counterexamples, ordinary contemporary scenes, source records, and my own selection history. The image is not asked to represent a culture by itself. It becomes evidence of how a model and an artist attempt to visualize culture.

I need epistemic metadata because technical metadata cannot describe uncertainty

A generated historical reconstruction may have perfect technical provenance. The system name is known. The time is known. The source assets are listed. But some parts of the reconstruction may be strongly supported by evidence and others may be invented for continuity. The file history does not automatically express this distinction. I therefore use Epistemic Metadata. Captured. Corroborated. Reconstructed. Inferred. Simulated. Fictional. Uncertain. These categories do not have to appear on every frame. They can be part of the work’s durable archive. Their purpose is not to destroy ambiguity. Their purpose is to prevent ambiguity from being mistaken for evidence after the work leaves its original context.

Context drift is the reason I care about provenance even in obvious art

Inside a museum, an invented archive may be clearly fictional. Outside the museum, a screenshot can circulate without wall text. A synthetic interview can be clipped. A fictional date can be reposted. A generated image can become a search result separated from its original title. I call this Context Drift. Artists cannot control every future misuse. But if a work deliberately borrows the grammar of evidence, I think it should preserve a path back to its production status. This can exist in end credits, websites, Research Archive pages, embedded provenance, and institutional documentation. The goal is not total control. The goal is recoverability.

I reject the idea that transparency means a giant label in the image

Transparency can be badly designed. A permanent watermark may make an artwork unreadable. A technical explanation before an emotional scene may destroy the very response the work wants to study. I therefore separate Experiential Transparency from Responsibility Transparency. Experiential transparency concerns what must be known during viewing. Responsibility transparency concerns what must remain available for ethical and epistemic accountability. A work can delay disclosure. It can let a spectator experience Synthetic Subjectivity before showing how the character was constructed. It can let a pseudo-archive feel authoritative before revealing the fictional production history. What matters is that the delay serves the artwork rather than concealing consequential relationships indefinitely.

My first strong counterargument: if the audience already knows the event is fictional, is the entire evidence experiment artificial?

Perhaps. A spectator who knows the event did not happen is not in the same situation as a journalist evaluating breaking news. I do not pretend the artwork reproduces real-world misinformation conditions. Its purpose is different. By removing the pressure to decide factual truth, I can isolate the aesthetic production of credibility. The spectator can notice how sound, framing, compression, interface, and testimony affect belief without being required to defend against actual deception. This is a laboratory of perception, not a substitute for media-literacy research. The limitation should remain visible. The artwork studies a component of trust, not the entire social problem.

My second strong counterargument: by making beautiful pseudo-evidence, am I contributing to the very visual vocabulary I criticize?

Yes, that risk is real. A convincing fictional archive can be copied precisely because it is convincing. This means formal success can increase circulation risk. My response is not to make the work visually weak. It is to build portable provenance and contextual seams into the work. The asset should retain enough information to recover its fictional status. The public release should avoid misleading captions. The Research Archive should clearly document the experiment. The artwork can test seductive evidence without presenting the seduction as real-world fact. This is what I mean by Accountable Fiction.

My third counterargument: powerful institutions can also sign false or misleading claims

A credentialed institution is not automatically truthful. A signed image can be selectively framed. An official source can be wrong. A trust list is an institutional arrangement, not divine authority. This is why provenance must enter an Evidence Ecology rather than becoming a master key. Independent sources matter. Contradictions matter. Historical context matters. Institutional incentives matter. A provenance credential is one relation among others. The artwork should teach neither blind skepticism nor blind verification. It should make relational judgment perceptible.

I am interested in “credential stratification”: the possibility that only powerful producers will look trustworthy

If future audiences learn to trust files with sophisticated credentials, well-resourced organizations may gain a new visual advantage. What happens to anonymous testimony? Old archives? Images from low-resource contexts? Copied media that lost its credential chain? A person documenting violence with outdated equipment should not become automatically less credible than a polished institutional asset. I call this risk Credential Stratification. The concept reminds me that provenance infrastructure must add a form of evidence rather than become the only form of evidence. For art, this means I should not aestheticize the verified badge as if technical polish were moral superiority.

The “verification interface” can itself become a fictional image

A green check mark can be generated. A provenance timeline can be generated. A screenshot of a trust interface can be generated. Once verification acquires a recognizable look, that look can be imitated. I distinguish Visualized Verification from Executable Verification. One looks like trust. The other can actually be checked. This distinction could become a separate work. One screen presents an impressive but fake verification dashboard. Another presents an ordinary file with a real inspectable provenance chain. The spectator’s first reaction is guided by design authority. Only later does the operational difference become clear. The work asks a very simple question: what happens when the image of verification becomes easier to produce than verification itself?

Synthetic witnessing changes sound as much as image

Evidence is often acoustic. A distant blast. A panicked call. A room tone. A voice with a particular spatial distance. Generated audio can reproduce the accidental qualities we associate with field recording. I therefore want a section of Evidence of Events That Never Happened to occur in darkness. The audience hears an apparently documentary sound field. After a long interval, it learns the sound was synthesized. Then it hears a real field recording from a real location. I do not ask viewers to guess which is which. I ask them to examine the emotional authority of Acoustic Testimony. Why does clipping feel accidental? Why does distant reverberation feel spatially truthful? Why does an imperfect breath feel like bodily presence? The collapse of visual evidence should not make me visually provincial.

Documentary cinema will not survive by banning synthesis; it will survive by clarifying the role synthesis plays

Documentary has long used animation, maps, reenactment, staged reconstruction, voice-over, editing, and archival montage. The ethical line has never simply been “camera equals documentary, fabrication equals fiction.” Generative tools join this longer history. The responsible question is functional. Is synthesis explaining a known relation? Reconstructing an incomplete event? Visualizing a witness account? Testing a hypothesis? Creating a fictional analogy? Simulating a future? I call this Functional Disclosure. Instead of saying only “AI was used,” the work should know what evidentiary role AI is performing. A generated reconstruction can be valuable in documentary practice if its uncertainty is preserved and its role is clear. A beautifully generated pseudo-witness should not silently inherit the status of direct testimony.

The archive of the future needs to preserve the difference between uncertainty and visual completion

Generative systems are excellent at filling gaps. Archives are full of gaps. This creates a dangerous fit. Missing architecture can be reconstructed. Damaged photographs can be completed. Unseen transitions can be generated. Unrecorded gestures can be animated. The temptation is to make the past coherent. But absence is historical information. If nobody photographed a room, that absence matters. If testimony conflicts, the conflict matters. If a body’s movement is unknown, the unknown matters. I use the phrase Uncertainty Fidelity to describe a formal obligation: the degree of uncertainty in the representation should remain proportionate to the degree of uncertainty in the evidence. A seamless image should not pretend to know more than the archive knows.

Negative Craft becomes an epistemic practice here

I usually describe Negative Craft as deletion, refusal, stopping, and withholding. In a synthetic-witness project, it becomes a way of protecting uncertainty. Do not generate the missing face. Do not complete the room. Do not make the testimony more fluent. Do not remove every contradictory detail. Do not stabilize the archival surface until it looks professionally restored. The work becomes stronger because it refuses to fill every gap. This is not anti-technology. It is a decision about what the work has earned the right to visualize.

I call that right “the boundary of permitted plausibility”

Every synthetic work has a plausibility budget. Some parts can be fully persuasive. Some should remain visibly provisional. Some should not be generated at all. I call this the boundary of permitted plausibility. The boundary is not fixed by a universal rule. It depends on the work. A fantasy world can be visually complete. A historical reconstruction built from weak evidence should preserve uncertainty. A synthetic character based on a real performer may require stronger consent and provenance. A cultural representation built from incomplete sources should resist smooth completeness. Authorship increasingly consists in drawing these boundaries.

The image that never happened can still tell the truth about how we believe images

This is why I continue to work with fictional evidence. I am not interested in winning a deception game. I am interested in making belief visible. The fictional phone video tells me something about why contingency feels trustworthy. The synthetic witness tells me something about why fluency feels human. The cultural portrait tells me something about why recognizability feels authentic. The fake verification screen tells me something about why institutional design feels authoritative. None of these are evidence of the fictional event. They are evidence of our evidentiary habits. This is the level on which art can contribute something distinctive.

Over the next three to ten years, I expect the visual surface to become the least reliable place to infer media origin

Models will improve. Camera simulation will improve. Physical consistency will improve. Audio will improve. Human viewers will become less able to infer production history from visual defects. This does not mean people become helpless. It means trust must migrate. Provenance. Institutional accountability. Independent corroboration. Epistemic metadata. Context. Media literacy. The visual surface will still matter aesthetically. It will simply do less of the authentication work by itself.

I do not want to become an artist of permanent suspicion

There is a danger in all this research. If every image becomes suspect, viewing can become paranoid. If every face is potentially synthetic, every testimony potentially generated, every archive potentially reconstructed, culture could lose the capacity to trust at all. I reject that outcome. My work is not training the spectator to believe nothing. It is training a slower question: What kind of relation supports this belief? Trust should become more articulated, not disappear. A real witness deserves to be heard without being required to look perfectly coherent. A reconstruction can be useful without pretending to be direct evidence. A fiction can be emotionally true without pretending to have happened. A generated cultural image can be studied without being treated as cultural fact. The goal is not disbelief. It is differentiated trust.

WHO IS LOOKING? becomes WHO CAN ANSWER FOR THE IMAGE?

The question connecting my current work is still WHO IS LOOKING? But synthetic witnessing adds a second question. Who can answer for the image? The camera operator? The model provider? The artist? The institution? The archive? The person whose identity was synthesized? The spectator who shares the image outside context? Responsibility is distributed, but it must not become invisible. I increasingly understand authorship as the organization of that responsibility. The artist is not the sole producer of every pixel. The artist is the person who must decide what kind of belief the work is permitted to request.

My next step is to build an “evidence score” that does not pretend to be objective

For the installation, I want each media fragment to have an internal production card. Not a public truth score. A working card for me. Capture status. Source status. Identity status. Reconstruction status. Uncertainty level. Potential context-drift risk. This card does not tell the audience whether to believe the image. It forces me to know what I am asking the image to do. I call this Evidence Ledger. It is a backstage tool, not an authoritarian rating system.

Final: an event does not need to have happened for an image to matter, but the image must not forget that it did not happen

That is the sentence I keep returning to. A fictional event can reveal real structures. A generated face can produce real emotion. A synthetic archive can expose real habits of belief. A nonexistent witness can force us to think more carefully about testimony. None of this requires pretending that the fiction was fact. In fact, the work becomes more powerful when it carries the difference. I want the image that never happened to remain visibly connected to its generation event, its sources, its uncertainty, its fictional domain, and the responsibility of the person who released it. Post-camera cinema should not compete with photography by becoming a better liar. It should use its new freedom to show how cinematic credibility has always been constructed, and then build new forms of accountability for a world in which photographic appearance no longer guarantees photographic presence. The ethical future of synthetic moving image will not be decided by whether artists stop fabricating. Artists will continue to fabricate. We should. It will be decided by whether we can distinguish invention from evidence without destroying the artistic power of either. That is the cinema I want to make after the witness: a cinema capable of creating impossible events, synthetic subjects, and unreal archives while remaining precise about the real relations that make those fictions possible.
