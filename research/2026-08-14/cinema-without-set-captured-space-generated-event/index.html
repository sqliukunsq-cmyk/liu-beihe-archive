Cinema Without a Set: From Captured Space to Generated Event

Cinema Without a Set: From Captured Space to Generated Event

作者 / Author：刘北河 LIU BEIHE 独立影像艺术家、导演 / Independent Moving-Image Artist & Filmmaker LIU BEIHE Research Archive / 刘北河研究档案

I began to understand the set only when I learned how to make an image without one

For most of my life as a filmmaker, space was something I had to negotiate before I could transform it into an image. A wall had a real distance from a performer. A lens could occupy one position and therefore had to surrender every other position. A window either existed or it did not. Light had a physical source, even when that source had been carefully fabricated. A room could be cheated, extended, partially built, painted, composited, or projected, but the production still had to construct a relation between a camera and some prior arrangement of things. The set did far more work for the director than I understood. It resisted. A wall would not move because my composition was weak. Weather did not reset because I had missed the take. An actor could disagree, misunderstand a direction, tire, hesitate, or discover something I had not planned. A location had acoustics, dust, legal restrictions, histories, neighbors, distances, gravity. Every supposedly inconvenient condition forced the film into an encounter with something that was not me. Generative moving image changes this relation. I can now produce a shot of a room without building the room, entering the room, or even defining the whole room. The shot can contain walls, depth, reflections, movement and bodies while the depicted space may have no stable floor plan outside the visible frame. A camera may appear to travel through a volume that no physical device ever occupied. A body can turn and reveal architecture that has been recomputed rather than uncovered. I call this condition Cinema Without a Set. By “set,” I do not mean only a studio construction. I mean the prior spatial situation to which an image once had to answer. Cinema without a set begins when photographic appearance can be produced without a corresponding photographic event in the depicted space. That is not a declaration of victory over the camera. It is a change in the ontology of the image and, more importantly for me, a change in the responsibilities of directing.

Post-camera cinema is not the death of the camera

I have been using the phrase Post-Camera Cinema to describe moving images that no longer require a prior optical capture event as the condition of their existence. The phrase can sound triumphalist, as if photography were an obsolete technology and generative systems had finally liberated cinema from the material world. I reject that reading. The camera remains one of the most powerful ethical devices in cinema precisely because it must occupy a position in relation to something that exists independently of my desire. Even a staged fiction contains a real event of staging. A performer actually crosses a floor. A camera actually receives light. A production actually passes through time. Joanna Zylinska’s Nonhuman Photography is useful to me because it already destabilizes the simplistic idea that photography is merely a human eye extended by a camera. CCTV, medical scanning, satellites and drones demonstrate forms of imaging in which human intention and human spectatorship are not the sole organizing principles. Her later book The Perception Machine pushes this transformation further into computational culture, asking what happens when seeing becomes inseparable from data, machine learning and automated image flows. Generative systems introduce another discontinuity. They do not merely automate looking at an external scene. They can synthesize the appearance of a scene without receiving light from that scene. This is why I distinguish between a photographic event and a photographic appearance. A photographic event is a causal encounter in time: an imaging system receives information from a situation that is occurring. A photographic appearance is the visible grammar we have historically associated with such an event: lens perspective, depth of field, motion blur, grain, exposure, handheld instability, compression artifacts, focus breathing, camera height. For most of film history, these two were strongly coupled. They could be manipulated, but the appearance usually began from some capture event. Generative imagery weakens that coupling at industrial scale. An image may now look photographed while no camera ever occupied the apparent position and no depicted event occurred before the image existed. Post-camera cinema begins in that gap.

Cinema has always fabricated space, so fabrication itself is not the new problem

It would be historically careless to say that generative cinema invents unreal space. Cinema was spatially dishonest almost from the beginning, and often brilliantly so. Méliès turned the studio into a theatrical machine in which substitution, painted scenery and stop-camera effects made impossible events photographically present. Classical studio systems built streets that ended behind facades. Matte paintings extended horizons. Rear projection placed performers in landscapes they never entered. Blue and green screens separated bodies from environments. Digital compositing made the final “location” a relation among many layers. Virtual production continues this history rather than abandoning it. LED volumes and real-time engines place a physically present camera and actor in relation to a computational environment. The technical challenge is precisely to make the physical and virtual systems behave as one coherent optical situation. Even current in-camera VFX documentation has to address problems such as depth-of-field mismatch between the real camera and the virtual camera. That technical problem tells me something philosophically useful: the virtual set is not free of geometry. It has to negotiate with the physical camera. Generative moving image can push beyond this. The new condition is not simply that the background is fake. It is that the image may precede a fully specified world. I call this Shot-Before-World. In traditional production design, even an incomplete set typically precedes the shot as a spatial plan. A room is designed. Doors, walls, surfaces and routes are decided. The camera then chooses how to reveal it. In a generative workflow, I can easily reverse the order. I generate one compelling shot first. Then, when I need a reverse angle, I ask what kind of room must exist for that first shot to make sense. A third shot produces another constraint. The world is reconstructed retrospectively around a sequence of successful images. This is seductive because it lets visual invention lead. It is also dangerous because a film can look as though it possesses a world while actually possessing only a succession of persuasive windows. The production design of post-camera cinema may therefore become less concerned with designing objects than with designing what must remain true across images.

A generated room is not necessarily a room

A physical room has resistance. Even a three-wall studio set has a measurable geometry that can be shared by the cinematographer, performer, sound recordist and production designer. A table may be moved, but it will not change scale because a new camera angle has been requested. If a door is one meter from a window, that relation remains available to everyone on set. A generated room can be closer to a temporary agreement. For one shot, the model stabilizes a set of relations among wall, window, body, table and light. A new shot can reopen those relations. Three windows may become two. A table may subtly change height. Light may appear to come from an architectural source that does not exist in the reverse angle. Current research on video generation and world models treats these failures as technical problems of temporal, geometric and physical consistency, and rightly so when the goal is simulation, robotics, or physically reliable prediction. But as an artist, I do not want to understand inconsistency only as a defect. It reveals something about the status of the generated environment. The visible world may be coherent locally without existing globally. I call this the Local World: a world sufficiently stable for the conditions of a shot but not necessarily obligated to possess a complete off-screen state. This is not identical to a false-back studio street. The street is incomplete, but its incompleteness is physically determinate. The wall ends somewhere. The generated environment may not merely hide its unseen regions; those regions may not yet be computed. That distinction changes off-screen space. Classical cinema has always depended on what lies beyond the frame. Sound, eyelines, entrances and exits make us imagine a larger world. In a Local World, off-screen space can become not an unseen extension but a future probability. For me, this is an extraordinary artistic problem. It means that the old question “What is outside the frame?” can become “Does outside the frame have a state yet?”

Alien Friend: nonhuman vision may begin with what a world chooses to preserve

This question enters directly into Alien Friend. I do not want to signify an alien point of view through the usual shortcuts: green color casts, thermal vision, fisheye distortion, HUD graphics, scanning lines. Those codes announce nonhuman perception while leaving the deeper cinematic hierarchy unchanged. The image may look unusual, but it still cuts to the face when the face matters, accelerates when danger appears, isolates the speaker when dialogue becomes important. I am more interested in changing what the image believes must remain stable. Imagine a character moving through a domestic interior. Color temperature remains consistent. Sound remains continuous. Surfaces are recognizable. Yet distances drift slightly between shots. A corridor is longer the second time. A doorway has moved a few centimeters relative to a window. No object melts, no wall visibly transforms. The viewer initially experiences only a weak bodily unease. If I can organize that drift rigorously, it stops being a random model failure and becomes part of a nonhuman spatial logic. I call intentional, conceptually organized instability Structured Alter-Space. The distinction matters. I do not want to romanticize every technical mistake. A model failing to preserve geometry is not automatically evidence of alien perception. The error becomes artistic only when I establish a repeatable rule connecting it to attention, memory, narrative and sound. What if the fictional observer preserves temperature more faithfully than distance? What if it remembers vibration but not object position? What if human faces are unstable while wall textures remain exact? The “alien” quality would then arise from a different hierarchy of preservation. This connects to a concept I have been developing for the work: Nonhuman Salience. The alien is not alien because it sees a strange color spectrum. It is alien because it assigns importance differently. Space becomes one way to materialize that difference.

The impossible camera movement is too easy; I want the almost-impossible camera

One of the most spectacular affordances of generative video is a camera that can go anywhere. It can pass through walls, bodies and objects. It can move from microscopic to planetary scale. It can reverse direction without inertia. It can occupy positions that no crane, drone, Steadicam or motion-control system could reach. These possibilities are initially exciting. They also become clichés very quickly. If every generative film demonstrates its freedom with a spectacular fly-through, the impossible camera becomes a new preset. I am more interested in what I call the Disembodied Camera: a camera whose lack of a physical body is not announced as spectacle but slowly discovered as a violation of embodied expectation. A thirty-second shot might appear completely conventional. Only after sustained viewing does the spectator realize that the perspective center has moved in a way no physical device could move, or that an occlusion relation has remained impossible, or that distance has changed without the optical consequences expected from a lens. The smaller the betrayal, the more interesting it becomes to me. I want the viewer to feel the loss of bodily ground before being able to name the technical trick. This is important because post-camera cinema should not become an exhibition of what cameras cannot do. Its deeper question is what happens to cinematic perception when the imagined camera no longer has to share our body’s physical constraints.

World models turn space into an engine of possible events

The recent language of “world models” and “world simulators” changes the problem again. A generated video is no longer necessarily conceived as a finished visual artifact. It can be treated as a provisional environment in which a system predicts how a scene may evolve under actions or conditions. Research in 2026 on physically faithful video generation, geometric consistency and reference-free physical assessment makes this engineering ambition very explicit. If generated video is to support robotics or Physical AI, it cannot merely look plausible frame by frame. State has to persist. Actions need consequences. Geometry needs enough consistency for a task. For film, this suggests a movement from represented space to what I call Computable Event Space. Traditional cinematic space contains events. A room is staged, and something happens inside it. A computable event space can participate in producing the next event. A character moves; the system must update state. A camera changes trajectory; the environment must continue. One visible moment becomes a condition for another. Harun Farocki’s concept of the operational image is crucial here. His operational images were not simply pictures of processes; they were embedded in processes of recognition, navigation and control. With a generative world model, the image may also become a surface through which possible futures are produced and evaluated. This changes the tense of the image. Instead of saying, “This was there,” the generated sequence can say, “Given these conditions, this is one possible next state.” That is not testimony in the photographic sense. It is a visual hypothesis. Cinema has always imagined possible worlds. What is changing is that possibility can now become computationally iterative. The director may increasingly design not only a sequence of shots but a field of permitted events.

Conditional directing is not prompt engineering

When people discuss directing with generative systems, the conversation often collapses into prompting. How precise is the prompt? Which words produce the desired lens? Which syntax controls movement? Prompting can matter, but I do not think it is the deepest transformation of directing. I call the larger practice Conditional Directing. The director defines which relations are locked, which may vary, which forms of inconsistency count as failure, which are meaningful, which real references may enter, which cultural materials must be excluded, what constitutes a valid continuation, and when generation must stop. In other words, directing becomes the design of a possibility space. A prompt is one condition among many. Reference images are conditions. Model choice is a condition. Character identity constraints are conditions. Camera trajectories are conditions. Sound is a condition. Provenance rules are conditions. Ethical limits are conditions. Negative instructions are conditions. Selection criteria are conditions. A post-camera director who merely requests “a beautiful cinematic scene” and selects a polished result may actually exercise less authorship than a traditional director with limited means. Automation can hide decisions inside platform defaults. Conditional Directing is my attempt to make those decisions visible again. The question is no longer only “What do I want to see?” It is also “What kind of world am I willing to permit in order for this image to exist?”

The strongest objection: cinema without a set may become cinema without an other

This is the most serious challenge to the entire idea. A physical set is not just geometry. It is a social situation. Actors disagree. Weather interrupts. A location has residents. Materials fail. Time runs out. A performer has rights and limits. A community may refuse access. A building has a history that exceeds the film. The world is not infinitely editable. Generative production can remove much of this friction. If a face does not work, regenerate it. If the weather is wrong, regenerate it. If the architecture constrains composition, regenerate it. If a synthetic performer does not obey, make another version. The filmmaker risks entering what I call Space Without Otherness: a visually rich environment in which everything ultimately responds to the author. That is not liberation in any simple sense. A world that obeys too easily can become aesthetically impoverished because nothing has the power to surprise the director except statistical accident. This is why I do not celebrate Cinema Without a Set as an ideal. I treat it as a condition that requires new forms of resistance. One strategy is hybrid production. Captured and generated material can confront each other rather than pretending to be interchangeable. Another is Negative Craft: restrict generation, preserve specific discontinuities, reject easy beauty, establish stopping rules, maintain a record of what was refused. A third is ethical constraint: determine which identities, cultural references or bodies should not become frictionless material simply because a model can render them. The more technically free the image becomes, the more deliberately the filmmaker may need to rebuild limits.

A second objection: am I simply turning technical failures into philosophy?

This objection is equally necessary. Current generative video often contains spatial drift because the systems are imperfect. If I call every inconsistency “ontology,” I risk using theoretical language to excuse weak production. In three years, many of today’s failures may be much less common. If my aesthetic depends on unstable hands, changing furniture or broken physics, the work will age with the bug. I therefore need to separate Accidental Inconsistency from Structured Alter-Space. Accidental inconsistency is a failure I did not design and cannot meaningfully connect to the work. Structured Alter-Space is a rule I can articulate, repeat, test and relate to the fictional subject or conceptual argument. This distinction allows my theory to survive technical progress. Even if future models maintain perfect 3D consistency, a generated room can still exist without a prior photographic set. Even if physical simulation becomes excellent, photographic appearance can still be decoupled from a prior capture event. Technology may solve the errors. It does not automatically restore the missing photographic event. In fact, greater realism will make the conceptual question more urgent. If a generated room becomes indistinguishable from a captured room, provenance, authorship and context become more important, not less.

Capture event, generation event, narrative event

To clarify this new temporal structure, I distinguish three kinds of event. A Capture Event occurs when an imaging device and a real situation meet in time. Light, sensor, lens, performer, location and recording process enter a causal chain. A Generation Event occurs when a computational system produces a visible sequence under specified conditions. A Narrative Event occurs when the spectator understands that something has happened in the world of the work. Traditional fiction often couples all three. An actor falls on a set; the camera records the fall; the story contains a fall. Post-camera cinema can separate them completely. No body falls. A generation process produces the appearance of a fall. The fictional world still contains a fall. This separation does not make fiction fraudulent. Fiction has never required factual occurrence. But the distinction becomes crucial when a work adopts documentary, archival, evidentiary or journalistic grammar. The question “Did this happen?” can refer to three different levels. Did the narrative event happen in the fiction? Did a generation event produce this image? Did a corresponding capture event ever occur? For future moving-image literacy, I think spectators will need to become more fluent in these layers.

Continuity may become world-consistency design

Traditional continuity departments track costumes, props, actions, eyelines, time of day and dialogue. Their labor protects the illusion that separate shots belong to one continuous world. Generative cinema expands that responsibility. Is the face still the same identity? Is the body the same scale? Is the room geometrically compatible? Does an object preserve state after being moved? Does the light still have a plausible source? Does the character remember what the previous scene established? Does a generated scar remain? Does a doorway continue to exist after it leaves the frame? I call the new task World-Consistency Design. The goal is not perfect consistency. Perfect consistency can itself become a default that removes useful strangeness. The important skill is to decide which things must remain stable and which may drift. Some discontinuities are errors. Some may express altered perception. Some may reveal the constructed nature of the world. Some may become narrative information. The director needs to distribute consistency rather than simply maximize it. This is one place where traditional production knowledge remains indispensable. Generative systems do not eliminate continuity; they make continuity a more explicit act of world construction.

Sound may become the last site of a site-less image

Discussions of generative cinema often become visually obsessed. But space is profoundly acoustic. A real room has reverberation. Materials absorb frequencies. Distance changes voice quality. A street has directional traffic. Wind, electricity, insects, ventilation and architecture produce spatial evidence that the eye cannot supply. This gives me a formal strategy I find very compelling: allow the image to be fully generated while the sound remains materially located. In Alien Friend, I could generate an environment that has no physical original and combine it with a long field recording from one real place. The image’s geometry may drift while the acoustic environment remains geographically continuous. The viewer receives contradictory evidence. The eye says the room is unstable. The ear says the place persists. The reverse is also possible. A visually stable generated room can be paired with changing acoustic scales so that the spectator loses confidence in the room’s dimensions. I call this the Acoustic Site. When the shooting site disappears from the image, sound can become a residual record that somewhere was actually entered, heard, measured, occupied. This does not restore a simple authenticity. It creates productive friction between different kinds of presence.

Ethnic Dolls: YAO proves that a cultural world cannot be treated as a free virtual set

The idea of a frictionless generative environment becomes especially dangerous when the space carries cultural identity. A model can rapidly synthesize an apparently coherent “Yao world”: clothing, silver ornaments, textiles, mountain landscapes, domestic architecture, ritual atmosphere. The result may be immediately legible and visually seductive. That coherence can be the problem. A living culture contains regional differences, histories, modern transformations, ordinary life, internal disagreement and details that do not function as identity signals for an outsider. Generative systems tend to overvalue the features that make the category recognizable. I call this Cultural Compression. In Ethnic Dolls: YAO, I therefore cannot treat the model as a neutral production designer. The generated environment has to be questioned as an interpretation. Which references come from which region? Which historical periods are being collapsed? Which visual signs appear disproportionately often? What has disappeared because it is not easily recognizable as “ethnic”? Who has the authority to say that an apparently beautiful world is actually a distortion? UNESCO’s work on AI and culture is useful here because it treats cultural diversity, creators’ rights, linguistic asymmetry and homogenization as structural concerns rather than decorative questions. For my practice, the implication is simple: “can be generated” is not equivalent to “should be generated.” Some cultural spaces may need to remain incomplete. Some missing material should remain missing. A no-set cinema must never become a no-source cinema.

The production site does not disappear; it becomes distributed

The phrase Cinema Without a Set can create another illusion: that generative production is somehow immaterial. It is not. Servers are material. GPUs are material. Electricity, cooling, data centers and networks are material. Training data contain prior visual labor. Character consistency requires design, testing and selection. Sound, research, legal work, software and post-production all involve people. What disappears is not production materiality but the concentration of relations in a single traditional shooting location. I call the new arrangement the Distributed Set. Part of the set may be a real recording location. Part may be a cloud computation. Part may exist in reference archives. Part may be the labor of remote artists. Part may be the field-recording site. Part may be the interface where conditions are defined. Part may be the gallery in which the audience finally experiences the work. This is important politically because the visual rhetoric of “AI made this” can erase distributed human and infrastructural labor. A mature post-camera cinema should resist the language of magic. The image may have no prior depicted set, but it still has conditions of production.

When the shooting site becomes weaker, provenance has to become stronger

A traditional production leaves many traces of how an image came into existence: camera files, call sheets, locations, set photographs, performance records, crew lists, time stamps, production notes. A generated image may lack some of these familiar anchors. That means post-camera cinema needs new archives. Which model was used? Which reference images entered the process? Was real footage included? Does a synthetic character derive from a real person? Was a real location replicated? Which sections are reconstructed? Which cultural references were used? Which version became public? What was deliberately rejected? C2PA and Content Credentials can help preserve parts of digital provenance, but technical provenance does not replace artistic responsibility. I have therefore been developing the idea of an Authorial Provenance Protocol: a practical record of the relationships that matter to the work, not merely a dump of settings. Cinema without a set should not become cinema without a history. The less a work depends on a traditional photographic site, the more actively I need to preserve the conditions through which it became visible.

The viewing site becomes more important when the shooting site disappears

There is a paradox I find increasingly productive. The image may lose its physical shooting set, but the spectator still has a body. Projection scale is physical. Speaker placement is physical. The distance from screen to viewer is physical. Darkness, architecture, seating, temperature and duration remain stubbornly material. I want to use this contradiction rather than hide it. Imagine a generated camera moving effortlessly through a wall while a real wall in the gallery forces the viewer to walk around it. Imagine a synthetic person with no physical original projected larger than a human body, forcing the viewer to tilt their head upward. Imagine a completely fictional room paired with sound coming from a real corridor behind the audience. I am interested in a three-screen structure: Captured Space — an environment produced through physical photography. Hybrid Space — captured and generated relations interwoven. Generated Space — a spatial image with no corresponding prior depicted site. I would not label the screens immediately. I would let the spectator discover differences in stability, sound, camera behavior and provenance, then reveal the production histories. In this sense, post-camera cinema may recover embodiment not at the shooting site but at the viewing site.

The second death of the director would be the disappearance of visible decisions

Generative tools do not automatically make the director more powerful. They can also make the director less necessary. If a platform chooses camera movement, lighting, continuity, performance rhythm, production design and edit logic automatically, the human may be reduced to providing a narrative intention and accepting a polished result. I call this possibility The Second Death of the Director. The phrase is deliberately different from older debates about auteurism or the theoretical death of the author. I mean a practical death: the disappearance of identifiable directorial decisions into optimization systems. Conditional Directing is my response. The director must make the boundaries explicit. What may vary? What must remain? Which visual defaults are rejected? When does generation stop? Which physical impossibility has meaning? Which cultural reference is too reductive? Which beautiful shot must be deleted because it solves the wrong problem? Authorship shifts from total control to accountable selection. The director is not the person who produces every pixel. The director is the person who can explain why this world was allowed to become this world rather than another.

I do not want a generated world that obeys me too easily

The deepest reason I continue returning to the physical set is not nostalgia. It is otherness. A real world contains relations that do not exist for the filmmaker. A location has a past. A body has rights. A community has memories. Weather follows no screenplay. A material can fail. If generative cinema removes every refusal, the filmmaker risks producing an enormous mirror. Everything becomes possible, but everything ultimately confirms the author’s desire. For me, the artistic future depends on resisting that mirror. Sometimes resistance will come from real bodies. Sometimes from provenance rules. Sometimes from cultural limits. Sometimes from an acoustic site. Sometimes from intentionally fixed constraints. Sometimes from refusing to correct a discontinuity. Sometimes from allowing another person to veto a digital use. Freedom becomes meaningful only when something can still say no.

What I expect over the next decade

Over the next three to ten years, generative video is likely to become longer, more controllable and more coherent. Geometric and physical errors will improve. Character consistency will become easier. Camera control will become more sophisticated. The technical shock of “AI video” will diminish. That will make the artistic problem harder. Today, model instability can accidentally create strangeness. Tomorrow, strangeness will need to be directed. Today, a synthetic room reveals itself through errors. Tomorrow, it may be indistinguishable from a photographed room. At that point, the central questions will move away from technical spectacle. What is the provenance of this world? Why does it exist? What relations were required to make it? What was excluded? Which images had a capture event? Which existed only as generation events? Who is responsible for the cultural and human materials inside them? What kind of attention does the space demand from the spectator? These questions are less glamorous than model capability. They are also more durable.

Cinema can lose the set, but it cannot lose the world

I do not want Cinema Without a Set to become a slogan for replacing production. I want it to name a historical condition precisely enough that I can work against its dangers. The moving image no longer needs a prior depicted scene. That freedom is real. So is the loss of friction that can accompany it. My task as an artist is not to choose between camera and model, real and synthetic, old and new. It is to construct relationships in which each form reveals the other’s limits. Captured space can remind generated space that the world resists. Generated space can remind captured space that photography was never identical with truth. Real sound can anchor an impossible image. A generated character can expose how cinema manufactures subjectivity. A physical gallery can give weight to a world that never existed. A provenance record can give history to an image without a shooting site. If post-camera cinema is to become more than a technical phase, it must learn how to remain answerable to bodies, histories, cultures and consequences even when the depicted world was never physically there. That is the version of Cinema Without a Set I want to pursue. Not a cinema liberated from reality. A cinema forced to invent new ways of being responsible to reality precisely because the old photographic guarantee is no longer sufficient.
