Working Vocabulary / Artistic Research

Research
Frameworks

A working vocabulary developed through LIU BEIHE’s moving-image practice and artistic research.

These terms are used as practice-based tools for making, editing and discussing work. They are not presented as universal scientific categories or claims of exclusive authorship over broader fields of inquiry.

Post-Camera Cinema

Post-Camera Cinema names a condition in which an image can retain the sensory and visual grammar of photography without requiring a prior photographed scene. It does not mean that cameras disappear. It means that the camera is no longer the only route through which a moving image can acquire photographic appearance, and that directing increasingly includes the organisation of generated possibilities as well as physical events.

Synthetic Witnessing

Synthetic Witnessing asks how an image can perform testimony, memory or evidentiary authority when no corresponding capture event took place. The framework separates the capture event, generation event and narrative event, allowing the work to ask what an image is claiming rather than reducing the question to whether it is simply “real” or “fake.”

Read: The Image That Never Happened →

Machine Seeing

Machine Seeing describes the perceptual, classificatory and probabilistic logics through which computational systems organise visual possibility. In the studio it is treated not as a claim that a model sees like a conscious subject, but as a way to examine how training distributions, interfaces, ranking systems and generation procedures shape what becomes visible before an artist makes a final decision.

Nonhuman Salience

Nonhuman Salience focuses on the system-level ranking of what appears important, recognisable or worth preserving. The directing problem is not merely to accept or reject an output, but to notice where a model has already concentrated attention: faces over relations, dramatic completion over waiting, recognisable signs over ambiguous context. The artist can then work with, against or around that default hierarchy.

Cultural Compression

Cultural Compression is a working artistic-research concept for examining how internally diverse cultural realities can be narrowed into a smaller set of high-probability, machine-legible signs. The framework does not claim to define a culture. It studies the image system: which signs recur, what forms of ordinary or contemporary life disappear, and how recognisability can become mistaken for cultural evidence.

Read: Ethnic Dolls: YAO and Cultural Compression →

Negative Craft / Refusal

Negative Craft locates authorship in deletion, delay, limitation, non-selection and accountable refusal under conditions of generative abundance. It is not a rule to generate less, nor a moral claim that restraint is inherently superior. The principle is closer to “generate with reasons”: when candidates become abundant, the artist’s refusals, revisions and responsibility can become as structurally important as production itself.

Read: After the Prompt — Judgment, Provenance, Refusal →

Visual Evidence & Provenance

This framework begins from a weakening shortcut: photographic appearance alone is increasingly insufficient to prove that a prior capture event occurred. Provenance therefore matters as media history—how an image was produced, transformed and circulated—but it is not a truth machine. The artistic question becomes how production history, context, institutional accountability and claims can enter the work itself, including through provenance montage and conflicting evidence systems.

Read: Cinema, Provenance and the Ethics of Synthetic Witnessing →

How the frameworks connect

Post-Camera Cinema describes the changed image condition. Machine Seeing and Nonhuman Salience examine how computational systems organise possibility and attention. Cultural Compression studies one consequence of that organisation in representations of cultural identity. Synthetic Witnessing and Visual Evidence ask what kinds of authority generated images can acquire. Negative Craft describes a practical response: authorship through judgment, refusal, arrangement and responsibility.
